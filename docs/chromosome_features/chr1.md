# Chromosome 1

> The behemoth of the human karyotype: 249 million base pairs encoding more protein-coding genes than any other chromosome, yet the last to be fully sequenced --- not completed until May 2006, three years after the Human Genome Project declared victory.

## Physical Properties

- **Length**: 248,956,422 bp (GRCh38), representing ~8.0% of the total nuclear genome. The largest human chromosome by a comfortable margin --- chromosome 2 trails by ~6 Mb.
- **Centromere**: Metacentric, positioned at ~123 Mb, dividing the chromosome into a short arm (p, ~125 Mb) and long arm (q, ~124 Mb). The near-equal arm ratio places it in **Group A** of the Denver classification alongside chromosomes 2 and 3.
- **Gene density**: Contains an estimated 2,058 protein-coding genes (GENCODE v44), making it the most gene-dense chromosome in absolute count. Gene density is approximately 8.3 genes per Mb --- above the genome-wide average of ~6.5/Mb but well below chromosomes 19 and 22.
- **GC content**: ~41.3%, close to the genome average of 40.9%. The chromosome spans the full isochore spectrum from L1 (AT-rich) to H3 (GC-rich), with a pronounced H3 island in the 1p36 region.
- **Cytogenetic bands**: 63 sub-bands at 850-band resolution (ISCN), the most of any chromosome.

## Genomic Architecture

- **Isochore structure**: Chromosome 1 is a mosaic of all five isochore families (L1, L2, H1, H2, H3). The telomeric 1p36 region is notably GC-rich, while the pericentromeric heterochromatin at 1q12 is deeply AT-rich --- one of the largest constitutive heterochromatin blocks in the genome (sometimes called the "1qh" region), composed primarily of satellite II and III repeats visible as a C-band.
- **Segmental duplications**: The pericentromeric region is a hotspot for segmental duplications (SDs). One-third of human-specific SDs reside within pericentromeric regions, and chromosome 1 contributes disproportionately. A human-lineage-specific pericentric inversion distinguishes human chromosome 1 from the chimpanzee homolog.
- **Repeat content**: ~45% interspersed repeats (LINE-1 enriched in q-arm Giemsa-dark bands; Alu enriched in GC-rich, gene-dense regions of 1p). The 1q12 heterochromatin block contains megabases of classical satellite DNA that is notoriously difficult to assemble.
- **Fragile sites**: Houses several common fragile sites including FRA1A (1p36), FRA1B (1p32), FRA1E (1p21.3), FRA1F (1q21), and FRA1H (1q42). The rare folate-sensitive fragile site FRAXA is on the X chromosome, but FRA1A at 1p36 is among the most frequently expressed common fragile sites in the genome.
- **Evolutionary breakpoints**: A pericentric inversion between 1p13 and 1q23 occurred after the human-chimpanzee divergence, making it one of nine major chromosomal inversions distinguishing human and chimpanzee karyotypes.

## Notable Genes & Loci

- **TP73** (1p36.32): The "other p53" --- a tumor suppressor and developmental transcription factor in the 1p36 deletion critical region. Unlike TP53, it encodes both pro-apoptotic (TAp73) and anti-apoptotic (deltaNp73) isoforms from alternative promoters, creating an internal antagonism.
- **MTHFR** (1p36.22): Methylenetetrahydrofolate reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme in the methyl cycle. The C677T polymorphism (rs1801133) is carried by ~10% of the global population homozygously and affects folate metabolism, homocysteine levels, and neural tube defect risk.
- **NOTCH2** (1p12): A key developmental signaling receptor. Mutations cause Hajdu-Cheney syndrome (acro-osteolysis, osteoporosis) and Alagille syndrome type 2 (bile duct paucity).
- **GBA1** (1q21): Encodes glucocerebrosidase. Biallelic mutations cause Gaucher disease; heterozygous carriers have a 5-fold increased risk of Parkinson disease --- the single strongest common genetic risk factor for PD.
- **MUTYH** (1p34.1): A base excision repair glycosylase. Biallelic mutations cause MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP), an autosomal recessive colorectal cancer predisposition.
- **NBL1** (1p36.13): Neuroblastoma suppression of tumorigenicity 1, a BMP antagonist in the DAN family. Maps within the 1p36 critical region for neuroblastoma.
- **PRDM16** (1p36.32): A transcriptional coregulator critical for brown fat differentiation and cardiomyocyte identity. Haploinsufficiency contributes to the dilated cardiomyopathy and left ventricular noncompaction seen in 1p36 deletion syndrome.
- **Largest gene**: RYR2 (1q43) spans ~791 kb encoding the cardiac ryanodine receptor. Mutations cause catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT), a cause of sudden cardiac death in the young.
- **Gene clusters**: The Fc receptor cluster (FCGR2A, FCGR2B, FCGR3A, FCGR3B) at 1q23 is a hotspot for copy number variation influencing autoimmune susceptibility. The RD gene cluster (1p36) contains regulin family members.

## Disease Associations

- **1p36 deletion syndrome**: The most common terminal deletion syndrome in humans (~1 in 5,000 live births). Features include intellectual disability, seizures, cardiomyopathy (especially LVNC), hypotonia, and distinctive facial features. Deletions are typically 1.5-10 Mb and almost always de novo.
- **1q21.1 recurrent microdeletion/duplication**: A genomic disorder hotspot mediated by flanking segmental duplications. Deletions associate with microcephaly, developmental delay, and congenital heart defects; reciprocal duplications associate with macrocephaly, autism, and schizophrenia. The ~20-gene interval includes NBPF family members and GJA5 (connexin 40).
- **Neuroblastoma**: Somatic 1p36 deletion is found in ~25-35% of neuroblastomas and correlates with advanced stage, MYCN amplification, and poor prognosis. The critical region at 1p36.31 likely harbors one or more tumor suppressors (CHD5, CAMTA1).
- **Gaucher disease and Parkinsonism**: The GBA1 locus at 1q21 is a nexus linking lysosomal storage disease to neurodegeneration. Over 300 pathogenic variants are known.
- **Cancer GWAS hotspots**: 1p13.2 (LDL cholesterol, SORT1), 1q32.1 (inflammatory bowel disease, IL10), 1p31.3 (obesity, NEGR1), 1q25 (prostate cancer).
- **Charcot-Marie-Tooth 2A**: Caused by MFN2 mutations at 1p36.22, one of the most common axonal CMT subtypes.

## Evolutionary History

- **Ancestral origins**: Comparative cytogenetics indicates that the ancestral eutherian karyotype contained a chromosome homologous to human chromosome 1 as a single syntenic block. This synteny is conserved across most placental mammals --- the cat, dog, and horse all retain a largely intact chromosome 1 homolog.
- **Pericentric inversion**: After the human-chimpanzee split (~6 Mya), a pericentric inversion occurred between 1p13 and 1q23 in the human lineage. This inversion is one of nine major karyotypic differences between humans and chimpanzees (the others include the chromosome 2 fusion and inversions on chromosomes 4, 5, 9, 12, 15, 16, 17, and 18).
- **Deep conservation**: Chromosome 1 synteny extends deep into mammalian phylogeny. Chromosome painting studies show that the ancestral mammalian karyotype (~100 Mya) had approximately 19 autosome pairs, with regions homologous to human chromosome 1 present as a conserved block.
- **Pericentromeric instability**: The 1q12 satellite block has expanded significantly in the human lineage relative to great apes, making it a site of ongoing structural evolution.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

- **G-quadruplexes**: Chromosome 1 harbors a high density of potential G-quadruplex-forming sequences (PQS), particularly in the GC-rich 1p36 region and near promoters of housekeeping genes. Over 300,000 PQS exist genome-wide; chromosome 1, as the largest and most gene-rich, contributes disproportionately.
- **1qh constitutive heterochromatin**: The 1q12 satellite block forms one of the most prominent heterochromatic structures in the human karyotype. It is visible by C-banding and varies enormously in size between individuals (a benign "heteromorphism"). This region is prone to multi-branched inter-chromosomal associations, particularly in ICF syndrome (immunodeficiency, centromeric instability, facial anomalies), caused by DNMT3B mutations that fail to methylate satellite DNA.
- **Nuclear territory**: Chromosome 1, despite its size, does not always occupy the nuclear periphery. Chromosome territory positioning is cell-type-dependent, but gene-dense chromosomes tend to localize more internally. Chromosome 1 often occupies an intermediate radial position.
- **Telomere lengths**: Telomere length varies by chromosome arm. The 1p telomere is among the shorter telomeres in the human genome and shows accelerated shortening with age in some studies.
- **Curvature and flexibility**: The AT-rich 1q12 satellite DNA has high intrinsic curvature and low persistence length, consistent with its role in constitutive heterochromatin formation and pericentromeric cohesion.

## Epigenetic Landscape

- **1q12 methylation**: The satellite II/III DNA at 1q12 is heavily methylated in normal somatic cells. Loss of this methylation (as in ICF syndrome due to DNMT3B deficiency) leads to dramatic chromosome decondensation, multiradial chromosomes, and genomic instability --- a striking demonstration that DNA methylation is structurally essential at constitutive heterochromatin.
- **Imprinted regions**: Chromosome 1 contains several imprinted or candidate imprinted loci, though it is not as densely imprinted as chromosomes 6, 7, 11, 14, or 15. DIRAS3 (1p31.3), also known as ARHI, is a maternally imprinted (paternally expressed) tumor suppressor frequently silenced in ovarian and breast cancers.
- **Replication timing**: The gene-dense, GC-rich 1p36 region replicates early in S phase, while the 1q12 heterochromatin is among the latest-replicating regions in the genome --- a textbook example of the correlation between GC content, gene density, and replication timing.
- **Chromatin states**: The Roadmap Epigenomics data reveal that chromosome 1 spans the full spectrum of chromatin states: active promoters at gene-dense 1p36, strong enhancers near developmental gene clusters, Polycomb-repressed domains at HOX-like regulators, and constitutive heterochromatin at 1q12.
- **CTCF binding**: The gene-dense regions of 1p are enriched for CTCF binding sites, consistent with the isochore model in which GC-rich domains serve as topologically associating domain (TAD) boundaries.

## Deep Cuts

- **Last chromosome sequenced**: Despite being chromosome number 1, it was the *last* human chromosome to have its sequence completed (Gregory et al., Nature 2006). The difficulty arose from its sheer size, the complex pericentromeric segmental duplications, and the intractable 1q12 satellite block. The consortium required 150 scientists across 28 institutions.
- **The "1qh" polymorphism**: The size of the 1q12 heterochromatic block varies up to 3-fold between individuals and is one of the most common cytogenetic heteromorphisms reported in prenatal diagnostics. It is entirely benign but has historically caused parental anxiety when flagged on karyotypes.
- **Chromosome 1 and schizophrenia**: The 1q21.1 locus is one of the most replicated structural variant associations in psychiatric genetics. Deletions increase schizophrenia risk ~8-fold; duplications increase autism risk ~3-fold. The same genomic interval, same mechanism (NAHR between SDs), opposite phenotypic directions.
- **Satellite DNA as a chromosome identity marker**: The 1q12 satellite III repeat is used in FISH-based prenatal diagnostics as a chromosome 1-specific identifier. Its massive, polymorphic nature makes it one of the most recognizable cytogenetic landmarks.
- **Cancer's favorite deletion**: 1p loss is one of the most frequent chromosomal arms lost across all human cancers. Beyond neuroblastoma, 1p deletions are recurrent in meningioma, oligodendroglioma (co-deleted with 19q in the canonical 1p/19q codeletion that defines oligodendroglial tumors and predicts chemosensitivity), colorectal cancer, and hepatoblastoma.
- **MTHFR cultural phenomenon**: The MTHFR C677T variant has become one of the most discussed genetic variants in functional and integrative medicine, often far beyond what the evidence supports. It is a genuine enzyme polymorphism with measurable biochemical effects, but its clinical significance outside of neural tube defect risk and hyperhomocysteinemia is frequently overstated.
