# Chromosome 14

> The immunoglobulin heavy chain chromosome -- where adaptive immunity is assembled, where lymphomas are born, and where reciprocal imprinting syndromes reveal the tug-of-war between maternal and paternal genomes.

## Physical Properties

- **Total length**: ~107.04 Mb (GRCh38), the second-largest acrocentric chromosome
- **Centromere**: Acrocentric -- small heterochromatic short arm (14p) bearing rDNA arrays, and a gene-bearing long arm (14q)
- **Short arm (14p)**: Contains nucleolar organizer region (NOR) with 45S rDNA tandem repeats, satellite DNA (satellites I, II, III, beta-satellite), and segmental duplications shared with all five acrocentric chromosomes
- **Long arm (14q)**: ~89 Mb of euchromatic sequence harboring all protein-coding genes
- **Group classification**: Group D (acrocentric), with chromosomes 13 and 15
- **GC content**: 40.9% -- close to genome average (~41%), substantially higher than chromosome 13 (38.5%)
- **Gene density**: ~10.1 genes/Mb, roughly average among autosomes -- a stark contrast to its acrocentric sibling chromosome 13
- **Gene count**: ~830 protein-coding genes (RefSeq), placing it in the middle tier for human autosomes
- **Cytogenetic bands**: 23 G-bands at the 400-band resolution; the distinctive pattern includes a prominent dark band at 14q24 and a pale terminal band at 14q32 housing the IGH locus

## Genomic Architecture

### Isochore Structure
Chromosome 14 shows a mixed isochore profile. The proximal long arm (14q11-q21) is relatively GC-poor and gene-sparse, while the distal arm (14q24-q32) is GC-richer and gene-dense. This gradient creates a functional asymmetry: the proximal arm is more heterochromatic and late-replicating, while the distal arm is transcriptionally active and early-replicating. The 14q32 region is particularly gene-rich, containing both the IGH locus and the imprinted DLK1-DIO3 domain.

### Segmental Duplications
- The short arm (14p) shares extensive segmental duplications with other acrocentric chromosomes, including homologous sequences that facilitate Robertsonian translocations
- The pericentromeric region of 14q11 contains segmental duplications harboring the T-cell receptor alpha/delta (TRA/TRD) locus, where V(D)J recombination generates enormous somatic diversity
- Interchromosomal duplications link 14q11 with paralogous regions on chromosomes 2 (TRB) and 7 (TRG)

### Repeat Content
- **LINEs**: Slightly below genome average, consistent with the moderate GC content
- **SINEs/Alu**: Near genome average, with enrichment in the gene-dense 14q32 region
- **Satellite DNA**: The short arm contains alpha-satellite (centromeric), beta-satellite (proximal junction), and satellites I/II/III in the pericentromeric heterochromatin
- The T2T assembly revealed that 14p contains approximately 3-4 Mb of rDNA arrays, though copy number varies dramatically between individuals (from ~10 to ~100+ copies per chromosome)

### Common Fragile Sites
- **FRA14B** (14q23): Aphidicolin-sensitive common fragile site; maps within the large FERM domain-containing protein gene FRMD6
- **FRA14C** (14q24.1): Coincides with a region of recurrent deletions in various cancers

### Evolutionary Breakpoints
A major evolutionary breakpoint at 14q11 corresponds to the boundary between the T-cell receptor locus and the ancestral syntenic block. This region shows evidence of multiple independent rearrangements across mammalian lineages.

## Notable Genes & Loci

### The Immunoglobulin Heavy Chain Locus (IGH)
- **Location**: 14q32.33, the most telomeric gene cluster on 14q
- **Size**: ~1.25 Mb spanning the entire V-D-J-C gene segments
- **Architecture**: ~123 VH gene segments (of which ~39 are functional), 27 DH segments, 6 JH segments, and 9 CH constant region genes (IgM, IgD, IgG3, IgG1, IgA1, IgG2, IgG4, IgE, IgA2)
- **Function**: The IGH locus undergoes V(D)J recombination in pre-B cells to generate antibody diversity, then class switch recombination (CSR) and somatic hypermutation (SHM) in germinal centers
- **Theoretical diversity**: The combinatorial and junctional diversity at IGH alone can generate >10^6 distinct heavy chains; combined with light chains, total antibody diversity exceeds 10^11
- **Clinical significance**: The most frequently translocated locus in B-cell malignancies. The IGH enhancer (Emu) acts as an oncogene activator when juxtaposed to proto-oncogenes via translocation

### T-Cell Receptor Alpha/Delta Locus (TRA/TRD)
- **Location**: 14q11.2
- **Unique nested architecture**: The TRD locus is entirely embedded within the TRA locus -- TRD V, D, J, and C segments sit between the TRA V and J segments. During T-cell development, commitment to the alpha-beta lineage involves deletion of the entire TRD locus by V-alpha to J-alpha rearrangement
- **Size**: TRA spans ~1 Mb with 54 V segments and 61 J segments; TRD is a compact ~60 kb insert

### The DLK1-DIO3 Imprinted Domain (14q32.2)
- **The most complex imprinted region in the human genome after 15q11-q13**
- **Paternally expressed**: DLK1 (delta-like 1, a Notch ligand), RTL1 (retrotransposon-like 1), DIO3 (type 3 iodothyronine deiodinase)
- **Maternally expressed**: MEG3 (maternally expressed gene 3, a long noncoding RNA), MEG8, RTL1-AS (RTL1 antisense), and the largest known cluster of microRNAs in the human genome -- over 50 miRNAs and one of the two major snoRNA clusters (C/D box snoRNAs)
- **Imprinting control**: The IG-DMR (intergenic differentially methylated region) between DLK1 and MEG3 is the primary imprinting control element; methylated on the paternal allele, unmethylated on the maternal allele
- **Disruption causes**: Kagami-Ogata syndrome (paternal UPD14) or Temple syndrome (maternal UPD14)

### SERPINA Gene Cluster (14q32.13)
- A tandem array of ~10 serpin serine protease inhibitor genes spanning ~370 kb
- Includes **SERPINA1** (alpha-1 antitrypsin, the most abundant serine protease inhibitor in blood; deficiency causes emphysema and liver cirrhosis), **SERPINA3** (alpha-1 antichymotrypsin), **SERPINA6** (corticosteroid-binding globulin), and **SERPINA7** (thyroxine-binding globulin)
- The cluster arose through tandem duplication and shows evidence of ongoing gene conversion between paralogs

### Largest Gene
- **NRXN3** (14q24.3-q31.1): Encodes neurexin-3, spanning ~1.6-1.7 Mb -- occupying nearly 2% of the entire chromosome. One of the largest genes in the human genome. Neurexins are presynaptic cell-adhesion molecules essential for synapse formation and neurotransmitter release. NRXN3 polymorphisms are associated with alcohol dependence, drug addiction, obesity, and autism spectrum disorders

### Other Notable Genes
- **FOXG1** (14q12): Forkhead box transcription factor critical for forebrain development; haploinsufficiency causes FOXG1 syndrome (congenital Rett-like encephalopathy with microcephaly and absent speech)
- **NKX2-1** (14q13.3): Also known as TTF-1/TITF1 -- master transcription factor for thyroid and lung development; mutations cause brain-lung-thyroid syndrome; also the single most useful immunohistochemical marker for lung adenocarcinoma and thyroid carcinoma
- **TSHR** (14q31.1): Thyroid-stimulating hormone receptor; activating mutations cause nonautoimmune hyperthyroidism; inactivating mutations cause congenital hypothyroidism
- **DICER1** (14q32.13): Encodes the RNase III endoribonuclease essential for miRNA biogenesis; germline mutations cause DICER1 syndrome (pleuropulmonary blastoma, thyroid cancer, ovarian Sertoli-Leydig tumors)
- **AKT1** (14q32.33): Serine/threonine kinase central to PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling; somatic mutations in Proteus syndrome (the disorder that likely affected Joseph Merrick, the "Elephant Man")
- **PRIMA1** (14q32.12): Anchors acetylcholinesterase to synaptic membranes

## Disease Associations

### Kagami-Ogata Syndrome (Paternal UPD14 / upd(14)pat)
- Caused by paternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 14, epimutation (hypermethylation of the MEG3/DLK1 IG-DMR), or deletion of the maternally derived 14q32.2 imprinted region
- **Pathognomonic features**: Bell-shaped thorax with "coat-hanger" rib configuration on chest X-ray, full cheeks with protruding philtrum, polyhydramnios, placentomegaly
- Additional features: abdominal wall defects (omphalocele), joint contractures, hepatoblastoma risk, developmental delay
- Molecular basis: loss of maternally expressed noncoding RNAs (MEG3, miRNA cluster) and overexpression of paternally expressed protein-coding genes (DLK1, RTL1)
- ~50% from UPD, ~25% from epimutation, ~20% from deletion

### Temple Syndrome (Maternal UPD14 / upd(14)mat)
- The reciprocal disorder: maternal uniparental disomy of chromosome 14
- Features: pre/postnatal growth retardation, muscular hypotonia, small hands and feet, truncal obesity, precocious puberty (in 76% of pubescent patients), adult short stature
- Phenotypic overlap with both Prader-Willi syndrome (hypotonia, obesity) and Silver-Russell syndrome (growth retardation)
- Molecular basis: overexpression of maternally expressed noncoding RNAs and loss of paternally expressed genes

### Lymphoma Translocations
Chromosome 14 is the most frequently translocated chromosome in B-cell malignancies, always involving the IGH locus at 14q32:
- **t(14;18)(q32;q21)**: Juxtaposes BCL2 to IGH enhancer; found in ~85% of follicular lymphomas and ~30% of diffuse large B-cell lymphomas. The translocation occurs during V(D)J recombination in pre-B cells -- RAG-mediated breaks on chromosome 14 meet an unidentified mechanism creating breaks on chromosome 18. Breakpoints cluster in the BCL2 major breakpoint region (MBR, 70%) or minor cluster region (MCR, 30%). Remarkably, t(14;18) cells are detectable in the blood of ~50-70% of healthy individuals, but only a tiny fraction ever develop lymphoma
- **t(11;14)(q13;q32)**: Juxtaposes CCND1 (cyclin D1) to IGH; diagnostic of mantle cell lymphoma
- **t(8;14)(q24;q32)**: Juxtaposes MYC to IGH; diagnostic of Burkitt lymphoma (the first human cancer linked to a specific translocation, 1972)
- **t(4;14)(p16;q32)**: Juxtaposes FGFR3/MMSET to IGH; found in ~15% of multiple myeloma, associated with poor prognosis

### T-Cell Malignancies
- Inversions and translocations involving 14q11 (TRA/TRD locus) are recurrent in T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia (T-ALL)
- **inv(14)(q11;q32)**: Juxtaposes TRA enhancer to TCL1 oncogene in T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia

### Robertsonian Translocations
- rob(13;14) is the most common Robertsonian translocation (1:1,300 population frequency)
- rob(14;21) is a less common but clinically significant variant, conferring risk for Down syndrome through unbalanced segregation

### Other Disorders
- **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency** (SERPINA1): Most common genetic cause of emphysema; the Z allele (Glu342Lys) frequency reaches 4% in Northern Europeans
- **FOXG1 syndrome**: Severe intellectual disability with absent speech, seizures, and stereotypies
- **DICER1 syndrome**: Predisposition to pleuropulmonary blastoma, thyroid tumors, and ovarian sex-cord stromal tumors

## Evolutionary History

### Ancestral Origin
Chromosome 14 corresponds to a single ancestral syntenic block that has been remarkably conserved throughout mammalian evolution. The Muller element corresponding to human chromosome 14 can be traced back to the eutherian ancestor.

### Mouse Synteny
Human chromosome 14 maps primarily to mouse chromosome 12, with additional segments on mouse chromosomes 14 and 6. The large NRXN3 gene maintains its enormous size and syntenic position in mouse, suggesting that its intron expansion predates the primate-rodent divergence (~75 Mya).

### Primate Correspondence
- **Chimpanzee**: Human chromosome 14 corresponds to chimpanzee chromosome 15
- **Gorilla**: Maintains the same syntenic block
- The acrocentric morphology is conserved across all great apes
- Comparative mapping shows that the DLK1-DIO3 imprinted domain at 14q32 is conserved across all therian mammals, with imprinting status maintained for >150 million years -- one of the most ancient imprinted regions known

### IGH Locus Evolution
The immunoglobulin heavy chain locus shows dramatic variation across vertebrates. In humans, the 9 constant region genes (encoding 5 isotypes) reflect a series of tandem duplications that occurred during mammalian evolution. The IgG subclass expansion (4 subclasses in humans) is specific to primates; mice have a different set of IgG subclasses that arose independently.

### The rDNA Array Paradox
Despite being the most highly conserved coding sequence in the genome (18S/28S rRNA), the rDNA arrays on 14p show the most rapid copy number evolution of any locus. rDNA copy number can vary from <50 to >400 copies across individuals, changes within a single generation, and shows concerted evolution across all five acrocentric chromosomes simultaneously -- a process driven by unequal crossover and gene conversion.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

### IGH Locus Chromatin Architecture
The IGH locus undergoes one of the most dramatic chromatin conformational changes in any cell type:
- In non-B cells: the locus is in a linear, compacted state with repressive H3K9me2/me3 marks
- In pro-B cells: the entire ~1.25 Mb locus contracts into a compact rosette structure, bringing distal VH segments into proximity with D-J segments for recombination. This contraction is mediated by the transcription factor Pax5 and the cohesin/CTCF architectural complex
- The locus spans nearly the entire distal tip of 14q and physically occupies one of the most telomeric positions of any human gene cluster, placing it near the nuclear periphery -- paradoxically, the same location associated with gene silencing, yet IGH is among the most actively rearranged loci in the genome

### G-Quadruplex Enrichment at IGH
The IGH switch regions (used for class switch recombination) are extremely G-rich on the non-template strand and form stable G-quadruplex structures in vitro. These G4 structures are thought to stabilize R-loops during transcription through switch regions, facilitating access by AID (activation-induced cytidine deaminase) for class switch recombination. The G4-forming potential at switch regions is conserved across mammals.

### Acrocentric Short Arm Architecture
Like chromosome 13, the T2T-CHM13 assembly resolved the complete 14p structure:
- Distal junction (DJ) with segmental duplications homologous to other acrocentric chromosomes
- rDNA array with variable copy number (typically 30-60 copies, each ~45 kb repeat unit)
- Proximal junction (PJ) with beta-satellite
- Centromeric alpha-satellite higher-order repeat array
- The alpha-satellite array on chromosome 14 uses a D14Z9 higher-order repeat unit that is distinct from chromosome 13's D13Z1

### Nuclear Organization
Chromosome 14 territories occupy an intermediate nuclear position -- neither as peripheral as chromosome 13 nor as central as gene-rich chromosomes like 19 and 22. However, the IGH locus specifically relocates to the nuclear periphery during B-cell development, where it associates with the nuclear lamina during the stages of V(D)J recombination.

## Epigenetic Landscape

### The DLK1-DIO3 Imprinted Domain
This is one of the most epigenetically complex regions in the human genome:
- **IG-DMR** (intergenic DMR): The primary gametic DMR, methylated on the paternal allele. This is one of only ~25 confirmed germline DMRs in the human genome
- **MEG3 promoter DMR**: A secondary/somatic DMR, methylated on the paternal allele; its methylation is dependent on the IG-DMR
- The domain contains the largest miRNA cluster in the mammalian genome (>50 miRNAs), all maternally expressed
- The C/D box snoRNA cluster (SNORD112-114) within this domain is one of only two large imprinted snoRNA clusters (the other is at 15q11, in the Prader-Willi region)
- **MEG3** (maternally expressed gene 3) was one of the first imprinted lncRNAs identified and functions as a tumor suppressor by activating p53

### IGH Epigenetics
- The IGH locus undergoes stepwise epigenetic remodeling during B-cell development
- Germline VH segments are marked by H3K9me2 (repressive) and progressively gain H3K4me3 and H3K36me3 (active) marks as they become accessible for recombination
- The IGCR1 (intergenic control region 1) between VH and DH segments acts as a CTCF-dependent insulator, preventing premature V-to-DJ joining
- Class switch recombination requires transcription through switch regions, producing sterile (non-coding) germline transcripts that are regulated by cytokine-responsive enhancers and promoters

### rDNA Epigenetics
- Active rDNA copies on 14p are characterized by: unmethylated CpG promoters, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, and association with UBF and RNA Polymerase I
- Silent copies carry: dense CpG methylation, H3K9me3, H3K27me3, and association with the NoRC (nucleolar remodeling complex)
- The rDNA methylation state is remarkably plastic and responds to nutritional status, stress, and aging

### Replication Timing
The 14q32 region containing both IGH and the DLK1-DIO3 domain shows allele-specific replication timing: the active (expressed) IGH allele replicates earlier than the inactive allele in B cells, and the paternal and maternal 14q32.2 alleles replicate asynchronously, consistent with their different imprinting states.

## Deep Cuts

1. **The nested receptor**: The T-cell receptor delta (TRD) locus is physically embedded within the T-cell receptor alpha (TRA) locus at 14q11.2 -- one locus inside another. When a developing T cell commits to the alpha-beta lineage, it literally deletes the TRD genes by recombining flanking TRA segments across it. This means every mature alpha-beta T cell has permanently sacrificed its ability to become a gamma-delta T cell -- an irreversible developmental decision encoded by chromosomal deletion.

2. **Healthy lymphoma carriers**: The t(14;18) translocation, which drives follicular lymphoma by activating BCL2, is detectable by PCR in 50-70% of healthy adults' peripheral blood. These individuals carry small clones of cells with the translocation but will never develop lymphoma. The translocation is essentially a benign somatic scar of V(D)J recombination errors that occur routinely during normal B-cell development.

3. **The imprinting mirror**: Kagami-Ogata syndrome (paternal UPD14) and Temple syndrome (maternal UPD14) are phenotypic opposites controlled by the same 14q32 locus. Paternal UPD causes overgrowth features (large placenta, polyhydramnios, abdominal wall defects), while maternal UPD causes undergrowth (small stature, growth retardation). This perfectly illustrates the parental conflict theory of imprinting: paternal genes promote fetal growth to extract maximal resources from the mother, while maternal genes restrain growth to preserve resources for future offspring.

4. **The coat-hanger sign**: Kagami-Ogata syndrome produces a pathognomonic chest X-ray finding -- ribs that angle sharply downward from the spine, resembling coat hangers. This distinctive pattern, caused by overexpression of DLK1 affecting rib cartilage development, is so specific that it can be diagnosed radiologically before molecular testing.

5. **DICER1 and the miRNA processing paradox**: DICER1 on chromosome 14 encodes the enzyme that processes all miRNAs in the cell. Germline loss-of-function mutations cause DICER1 syndrome -- but the tumors that arise (pleuropulmonary blastoma, thyroid cancer) always retain one functional DICER1 allele plus a specific somatic missense mutation in the RNase IIIb catalytic domain. Complete DICER1 loss is lethal to cells. The tumors depend on a precise level of DICER1 dysfunction -- too much function and the tumor suppression works; too little and the cell cannot survive.

6. **The alpha-1 antitrypsin paradox**: The Z allele of SERPINA1 (Glu342Lys) causes alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency -- but it reaches 4% allele frequency in Northern Europeans, far too high for a deleterious allele under neutral drift. One hypothesis is heterozygote advantage: Z-allele carriers may have enhanced inflammatory responses that provided protection against historical infectious diseases, paralleling the sickle-cell/malaria story.

7. **Burkitt's triple milestone**: The t(8;14) translocation in Burkitt lymphoma holds three firsts in cancer biology -- it was the first cancer associated with a specific virus (EBV, 1964), the first associated with a specific chromosomal translocation (1972), and the first where the translocation target was identified as an oncogene (MYC, 1982). All three discoveries centered on chromosome 14.

8. **The most ancient imprinting**: The DLK1-DIO3 domain at 14q32 has maintained its imprinted status across all therian mammals for over 150 million years, making it one of the most evolutionarily ancient imprinted regions. The RTL1 gene within it is derived from a Ty3/Gypsy retrotransposon that was "domesticated" by the mammalian genome -- a viral gene that became essential for placental development.
