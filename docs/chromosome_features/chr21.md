# Chromosome 21

> The smallest human autosome -- a gene-poor, repeat-rich sliver of the genome whose very barrenness is what makes trisomy 21 the only autosomal trisomy routinely compatible with long-term survival.

## Physical Properties

- **Total length**: ~46.7 Mb (GRCh38), representing ~1.5% of the total DNA in cells. The long arm (21q) contains ~33.5 Mb of euchromatic sequence; the short arm (21p) is ~13 Mb of heterochromatic satellite DNA and ribosomal RNA gene arrays
- **Centromere type**: Acrocentric -- the centromere sits very close to the p-arm terminus, and the short arm consists entirely of repetitive sequences (rDNA arrays, satellite DNA). Chr21 is one of five human acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22)
- **Group classification**: Group G (chromosomes 21-22 and Y), the smallest autosomes
- **GC content**: ~41%, approximately equal to the genome-wide average. However, the distribution is highly heterogeneous: gene-rich subregions at 21q22 are GC-rich (>45%), while the large gene deserts are AT-rich (<40%)
- **Gene density**: Among the lowest of any human chromosome. Only ~240 protein-coding genes on the entire euchromatic long arm -- approximately 7 genes per Mb, compared to the genome average of ~10 and chr19's ~25. After chr13, it has the fewest protein-coding genes of any autosome
- **Non-coding RNA content**: 144 pseudogenes and >200 RNA genes (lncRNAs, miRNAs, antisense RNAs). Notably, chr21 has the second-highest ratio of lncRNA genes to protein-coding genes (36.6%) after chr18

## Genomic Architecture

### The Gene Desert Landscape
The defining architectural feature of chr21 is its vast gene-poor regions:
- A single 7 Mb stretch on 21q contains only one gene -- one of the largest gene deserts in the human genome
- Three additional 1 Mb regions on 21q are completely devoid of genes
- Together, these gene-poor regions comprise ~10 Mb, or approximately one-third of the euchromatic long arm
- The gene deserts correspond to Giemsa-dark (G-band positive) regions with low GC content (L isochores, <43% GC), where gene density drops to ~1 per 300 kb

### Isochore Structure
Chromosome 21 has a bimodal isochore distribution. The distal portion of 21q (band 21q22) is GC-rich and gene-dense, while the proximal and central portions are AT-rich and gene-poor. This creates a striking gradient: nearly all medically important genes cluster in the distal third of the long arm.

### Short Arm Architecture -- The rDNA Factory
The short arm (21p) is entirely heterochromatic and contains:
- **Ribosomal DNA arrays**: Tandem repeats of the 45S rDNA unit (~13 kb per repeat, containing 18S, 5.8S, and 28S rRNA genes). The nucleolar organizer region (NOR) on 21p is one of five human NORs (on chr13, 14, 15, 21, 22)
- **Satellite DNA**: Including satellite 1, satellite 3, and beta-satellite sequences in the proximal short arm
- **No protein-coding genes**: The entire short arm is repeat-derived and does not contribute to the euchromatic gene count
- The T2T consortium's completion of all acrocentric short arms revealed that these regions are far more structured than previously thought, with segmental duplications shared between all five acrocentric chromosomes

### Repeat Content
- **Total repeat content**: High, driven by the short arm satellite arrays
- **LINE-1 elements**: Enriched in the gene-poor AT-rich regions of 21q -- consistent with the general pattern of LINE accumulation in dark G-bands
- **Alu elements**: Depleted relative to genome average, reflecting the low GC content of much of the chromosome
- **Large genes dominate available space**: DSCAM spans >840 kb, GRIK1 >400 kb, and several other genes exceed 200 kb. The oversized genes partially explain the low gene density -- much of the euchromatic sequence is occupied by enormous introns

### Segmental Duplications
The pericentromeric region and subtelomeric zones of 21q show segmental duplications shared with other chromosomes. These duplications facilitate Robertsonian translocations between chr21 and other acrocentric chromosomes (particularly chr14), which account for ~4% of Down syndrome cases.

## Notable Genes & Loci

### APP -- Amyloid Precursor Protein (21q21.3)
The gene spans ~240 kb with 18 exons and encodes the transmembrane protein whose proteolytic processing generates amyloid-beta (A-beta) peptides -- the primary component of senile plaques in Alzheimer's disease. Trisomy 21 means three copies of APP, driving early-onset Alzheimer's in virtually all individuals with Down syndrome by age 40-50. Critically, recent work shows that trisomy 21 enhances A-beta deposition even independently of APP gene dosage, suggesting that other chr21 genes amplify amyloid pathology.

### DYRK1A -- Dual-Specificity Tyrosine Phosphorylation-Regulated Kinase 1A (21q22.13)
Located in the Down Syndrome Critical Region (DSCR), DYRK1A bridges beta-amyloid production and tau phosphorylation. It phosphorylates APP (altering stability and A-beta generation) and tau (promoting neurofibrillary tangle formation). DYRK1A overexpression from trisomy 21 is considered a key driver of the intellectual disability and neurodegeneration in Down syndrome. DYRK1A inhibitors are in development as potential therapeutics for both DS cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease.

### SOD1 -- Superoxide Dismutase 1 (21q22.11)
Encodes Cu/Zn superoxide dismutase, the primary cytoplasmic free radical scavenger. Gain-of-function mutations cause ~20% of familial ALS (amyotrophic lateral sclerosis / motor neuron disease). Over 180 pathogenic SOD1 mutations are known. Trizivudine (antisense oligonucleotide) targeting SOD1 mRNA was FDA-approved for SOD1-ALS in 2023. In DS, the extra copy of SOD1 was once proposed to cause oxidative stress through peroxide overproduction, though this "oxidative stress" hypothesis has been significantly refined.

### RUNX1 -- Runt-Related Transcription Factor 1 (21q22.12)
A master regulator of hematopoiesis. RUNX1 is the gene disrupted by the t(8;21)(q22;q22) translocation that creates the RUNX1-RUNX1T1 (AML1-ETO) fusion -- found in ~12% of de novo AML and up to 40% of AML-M2 (FAB classification). The t(8;21) is generally associated with favorable prognosis. Children with Down syndrome have a 10-20 fold increased risk of acute leukemia, partly attributable to RUNX1 dosage effects.

### DSCAM -- Down Syndrome Cell Adhesion Molecule (21q22.2)
One of the largest genes in the human genome, spanning >840 kb. DSCAM encodes a cell adhesion molecule critical for axon guidance, dendritic self-avoidance, and neural circuit formation. In Drosophila, DSCAM generates >38,000 splice variants through alternative exon usage -- more protein diversity from a single gene than any other known. The human version is less extreme but remains important for neural development, and its triplication contributes to DS neurological phenotypes.

### The Down Syndrome Critical Region (DSCR) at 21q22.1-22.3
A ~5.4 Mb region at the distal end of 21q that contains ~30 genes considered most likely to contribute to core DS phenotypes. Key DSCR genes include:
- **DYRK1A**: Intellectual disability, neurodegeneration
- **KCNJ6 (GIRK2)**: Potassium channel, GABAergic neurotransmission
- **RCAN1 (DSCR1)**: Calcineurin inhibitor (calcipressin family), cardiac and neurological effects
- **ETS2**: Transcription factor, skeletal abnormalities
- **COL6A1/COL6A2**: Collagen VI, connective tissue features

### DNMT3L -- DNA Methyltransferase 3-Like (21q22.3)
A catalytically inactive member of the DNMT3 family that stimulates de novo methylation by DNMT3A and DNMT3B. DNMT3L is essential for establishing maternal genomic imprints and for methylation-based retrotransposon silencing in the male germline. Its triplication in DS may contribute to the genome-wide epigenetic dysregulation observed in trisomy 21.

### CBS -- Cystathionine Beta-Synthase (21q22.3)
Homozygous deficiency causes homocystinuria, while the extra copy in DS may paradoxically protect against atherosclerosis through altered homocysteine metabolism -- one possible explanation for the reduced incidence of solid tumors (other than leukemia) in DS individuals.

## Disease Associations

### Down Syndrome (Trisomy 21)
The most common chromosomal aneuploidy in live births (~1:700). Phenotypic features include intellectual disability, characteristic facial features, congenital heart defects (particularly atrioventricular septal defects, ~40-50%), increased leukemia risk, and early-onset Alzheimer's. Approximately 95% of cases are full trisomy (meiotic nondisjunction), 4% are Robertsonian translocation (most commonly rob(14;21)), and 1% are mosaic.

**Why trisomy 21 is viable when most trisomies are not**: The key factor is chromosome 21's extraordinarily low gene content. With only ~240 protein-coding genes -- the fewest of any autosome except chr13 -- the dosage imbalance from triplication is smaller than for any other autosome. Even so, ~80% of trisomy 21 conceptions end in spontaneous abortion. The cumulative excess expression of dosage-sensitive protein complex subunits is the major source of cellular stress, and chr21's sparse gene content keeps this burden below the viability threshold.

### Cancer
- **Acute megakaryoblastic leukemia (AMKL)**: Children with DS have a 500-fold increased risk. GATA1 mutations are found in virtually all DS-AMKL cases, and the combination of GATA1 mutation + trisomy 21 is necessary and sufficient for the transient myeloproliferative disorder that precedes AMKL in DS neonates
- **Acute lymphoblastic leukemia**: 20-fold increased risk in DS
- **t(8;21) AML**: RUNX1-RUNX1T1 fusion, 12% of de novo AML
- **Solid tumors**: Paradoxically, DS individuals have significantly reduced rates of most solid tumors. The "protective" genes may include DYRK1A (anti-angiogenic via VEGF suppression), ETS2 (tumor suppressor activity), and RCAN1 (calcineurin pathway modulation)

### Neurological
- **Alzheimer's disease**: Near-universal by age 40-50 in DS, driven by APP triplication
- **ALS**: SOD1 mutations (gain of function, dominant)
- **Intellectual disability**: Core feature of DS, attributed to DYRK1A, DSCAM, KCNJ6, and other DSCR genes

### Structural Variants
- **Robertsonian translocations**: rob(14;21) is the most common, accounting for ~4% of DS cases. These arise from fusion of acrocentric short arms and can be inherited (carrier prevalence ~1:1,000)
- **Isochromosome 21q**: Rare, with only the long arm duplicated
- **Partial trisomy 21**: Cases where only part of 21q is triplicated have been critical for mapping genotype-phenotype correlations and refining the DSCR

## Evolutionary History

### Ancient Origins
The association of human chromosomes 3 and 21 (HSA3+21) is ancestral for placental mammals, dating back >100 million years. In many non-primate mammals (including Carnivora, Artiodactyla, and Scandentia), the regions homologous to chr3 and chr21 remain together on a single chromosome. The chr3/21 fission occurred specifically in the catarrhine (Old World monkey + ape) lineage, after the divergence from platyrrhines (New World monkeys).

### Mouse Synteny
Human chr21 genes map predominantly to mouse chromosome 16 (Mmu16), with additional syntenic segments on Mmu17 and Mmu10. The Ts65Dn mouse model of Down syndrome carries an extra copy of a segment of Mmu16 that is syntenic to much of the DSCR on human chr21. More refined mouse models (Dp(16)1Yey, Ts1Rhr) have been created to dissect which specific genes contribute to DS phenotypes.

### Bovine Conservation
Studies mapping genes between human chr21, mouse chr16, and bovine chromosomes confirmed that all chr21 homologs in cattle sit on a single chromosome, supporting the ancestral nature of the syntenic block.

### Chromosomal Stability in Primates
Chromosomes orthologous to HSA21 were not rearranged within the primate lineage -- the gene content and order have been stable since the emergence of primates. This conservation extends even to lemurs (>74 Mya divergence).

### Acrocentric Chromosome Evolution
The five human acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) share homologous sequences in their short arms, including rDNA arrays and satellite DNA. These shared sequences facilitate inter-chromosomal recombination, driving Robertsonian translocations and the evolutionary mobility of NORs. The short arm sequences undergo concerted evolution -- homogenization within species, divergence between species.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

### AT-Rich Mechanical Properties
The predominantly AT-rich gene deserts of 21q have distinctive biophysical characteristics:
- **Lower persistence length**: AT-rich DNA is more flexible (Lp ~49 nm at 30% GC vs. ~65 nm at 50% GC for unmethylated DNA), facilitating nucleosome wrapping
- **Lower melting temperature**: Reduced thermodynamic stability from fewer hydrogen bonds per base pair
- **Intrinsic curvature**: AT-rich sequences, particularly A-tract (A4-A6) motifs, adopt intrinsic curvature that can facilitate nucleosome positioning. The gene deserts of chr21 may have higher intrinsic curvature than gene-rich regions
- **AA/TT periodicity**: The nucleosome-positioning signal (10.5 bp periodicity of AA/TT dinucleotides) may be more prominent in the AT-rich deserts, contributing to regular nucleosome arrays

### The DSCAM Giant
At >840 kb, DSCAM is one of the largest genes in the human genome. Its enormous introns (several >100 kb each) present biophysical challenges:
- Transcription takes hours to traverse the gene
- The DNA must remain accessible through multiple rounds of replication during a single transcription event
- R-loops (RNA:DNA hybrids) formed during transcription of such large genes are potential sources of genomic instability

### Acrocentric Short Arm Structure
The 21p short arm harbors the rDNA NOR, where active rRNA transcription by RNA Polymerase I drives nucleolar formation. The rDNA arrays adopt specialized chromatin structures:
- Active rDNA units are largely unmethylated and depleted of canonical nucleosomes, instead associated with the upstream binding factor (UBF) and SL1
- Silent rDNA units are heterochromatinized by NoRC (nucleolar remodeling complex), DNA methylated, and H3K9me3-marked
- The ratio of active to silent copies varies between individuals and changes with aging

### G-Quadruplex Content
Despite overall moderate GC content, the gene-rich 21q22 region contains G-quadruplex-forming sequences at promoters, particularly in regulatory regions of DYRK1A, RUNX1, and other DSCR genes. These G4 structures may be relevant to the dosage sensitivity of these genes, as G4 formation can regulate both transcription initiation and mRNA translation.

## Epigenetic Landscape

### Trisomy 21 Methylation Signature
Genome-wide studies reveal a highly reproducible DNA methylation signature in Down syndrome:
- **8,624 CpGs hypermethylated** and 1,447 hypomethylated in DS fetal cortex (1.85% vs. 0.31% of analyzed CpGs) -- a strong bias toward hypermethylation
- **652 epigenome-wide significant CpGs** in newborn blood, with 1,052 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) distributed across all chromosomes, not just chr21
- The DS methylation signature has both tissue-specific and pan-tissue components, indicating both local and trans-acting effects of the extra chromosome
- DNMT3L triplication (21q22.3) may contribute to global hypermethylation by stimulating DNMT3A/3B activity

### Replication Timing
The gene-dense 21q22 region replicates early in S-phase, while the gene deserts replicate late -- consistent with the general isochore/gene-density/replication-timing correlation. The short arm rDNA arrays have a unique replication program tied to their nucleolar function.

### Chromatin States
- **21q22 (gene-rich)**: Enriched for H3K4me3, H3K27ac, active enhancers, and open chromatin (DHS)
- **21q11-q21 (gene deserts)**: Predominantly heterochromatic, with H3K9me3 and late replication
- **21p (short arm)**: Specialized nucleolar chromatin at active rDNA; constitutive heterochromatin elsewhere

### No Known Imprinted Genes -- Until DSCAM
Chromosome 21 was long considered to have no imprinted genes. However, a 2018 genome-wide screen identified DSCAM as the first imprinted gene on chr21, showing preferential maternal expression in the human placenta. This is particularly intriguing given DSCAM's role in DS neurological phenotypes -- parent-of-origin effects could modulate DS severity.

### Trans-Chromosomal Epigenetic Effects
The extra chr21 in DS causes methylation and expression changes genome-wide, not just on chr21 itself. Single-cell studies show that the transcriptional response to trisomy is heterogeneous -- not all cells upregulate chr21 genes to the same degree -- suggesting stochastic epigenetic buffering mechanisms that partially compensate for dosage imbalance.

## Deep Cuts

1. **Trisomy 21 is lethal -- just less so**: Approximately 80% of trisomy 21 conceptions end in spontaneous abortion, mostly in the first trimester. The 20% that survive to birth represent a highly selected subpopulation. Among live-born trisomies, only trisomy 21 permits survival into adulthood (trisomy 13 and 18 have median survival of days to weeks). The reason is purely quantitative: chr21 has the fewest genes, so tripling them produces the smallest absolute increase in protein complex subunit imbalance.

2. **The Down syndrome cancer paradox**: DS individuals have a 500-fold increased risk of AMKL and a 20-fold increase in ALL, yet a significantly reduced risk of nearly all solid tumors (breast, lung, colon, melanoma). The "tumor suppressor trisomy" hypothesis proposes that extra copies of DYRK1A (suppresses VEGF-driven angiogenesis), ETS2, and RCAN1 (calcineurin inhibition) collectively create a hostile environment for solid tumor growth. This has inspired drug development: DYRK1A inhibitors and calcineurin modulators are being explored as anti-cancer agents inspired by DS biology.

3. **Chromosome 21 was the second chromosome fully sequenced**: Published in Nature in May 2000 (Hattori et al.), chr21 was sequenced to near-completion one year after chr22. The chr21 sequence was a milestone for DS research, but it also revealed a surprise: only 127 known genes on the entire chromosome. The predicted gene count was later revised upward to ~240, but the "gene poverty" finding transformed thinking about DS pathogenesis -- with so few genes, identifying the causative ones seemed achievable. Two decades later, it remains incomplete.

4. **The 7 Mb desert**: A single continuous 7 Mb stretch on 21q contains only one gene. This is one of the largest gene deserts in the human genome. These deserts are not functionless -- they contain long-range regulatory elements (enhancers) that control genes at megabase distances, and they are enriched for conserved non-coding elements. The deserts also serve as "buffers" that insulate gene regulatory domains from each other.

5. **DSCAM: the most diverse gene on Earth (in flies)**: In Drosophila, the DSCAM gene generates >38,000 distinct mRNA isoforms through combinatorial alternative splicing of four exon clusters -- more protein diversity from a single gene than any other known. Each neuron expresses a unique combination of DSCAM isoforms, creating a molecular "barcode" for self-recognition and self-avoidance. Human DSCAM is less extreme but retains the self-avoidance function. And it was recently discovered to be imprinted in the placenta.

6. **The transient leukemia that cures itself**: Approximately 10% of DS neonates develop transient myeloproliferative disorder (TMD) -- a pre-leukemic expansion of megakaryoblasts bearing GATA1 mutations. In ~80% of cases, TMD spontaneously resolves within 3 months as the fetal liver hematopoietic program shuts down. But ~20% of TMD cases progress to full AMKL, typically by age 2-4. This natural history -- a leukemia that arises, regresses, and sometimes returns -- is one of the most remarkable examples of developmental stage-dependent oncogenesis.

7. **The Robertsonian translocation trap**: Rob(14;21) carriers (~1:1,000 people) appear chromosomally normal (they have 45 chromosomes, with the fused 14;21 doing double duty). But they have a significant risk of producing offspring with translocation Down syndrome. A female carrier has ~10-15% risk per pregnancy; a male carrier ~1-3% (the asymmetry reflects meiotic selection against unbalanced sperm). This means Down syndrome can "run in families" through translocation carriers -- something many people find surprising.

8. **Chr21 protects against atherosclerosis**: Despite the metabolic complications of DS (obesity, hypothyroidism, diabetes risk), DS individuals have remarkably low rates of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. The CBS gene (21q22.3) may contribute by lowering homocysteine levels (homocysteinuria, caused by CBS deficiency, accelerates atherosclerosis). The extra copy of CBS in DS creates a metabolic environment hostile to plaque formation -- an accidental cardioprotective trisomy.

9. **The NOR lottery**: The rDNA arrays on 21p (and the other four acrocentric short arms) vary enormously between individuals in copy number, methylation status, and activity. Some individuals have active NORs on all 10 acrocentric chromosomes; others have active NORs on only 6-7. The number of active NORs decreases with age, and this decline correlates with decreased ribosome biogenesis -- potentially linking chr21 short arm biology to the aging process itself.
