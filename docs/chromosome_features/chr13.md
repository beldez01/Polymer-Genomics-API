# Chromosome 13

> The genome's quiet desert -- gene-poor, repeat-rich, and home to two of the most iconic tumor suppressors in cancer biology.

## Physical Properties

- **Total length**: ~114.36 Mb (GRCh38), making it the largest acrocentric chromosome in the human karyotype
- **Centromere**: Acrocentric -- centromere positioned near one end, with a cytogenetically invisible short arm (13p) and a long euchromatic arm (13q)
- **Short arm (13p)**: ~16 Mb of heterochromatic sequence including rDNA arrays, satellite DNA, and segmental duplications; fully sequenced for the first time in the T2T-CHM13 assembly (2022)
- **Long arm (13q)**: Contains essentially all protein-coding genes
- **Group classification**: Group D (acrocentric), along with chromosomes 14 and 15
- **GC content**: 38.5% -- substantially below the genome-wide average of ~41%, consistent with gene poverty and L1/L2 isochore dominance
- **Gene density**: 6.5 genes/Mb, among the lowest of any human autosome. A central 38 Mb region drops to just 3.1 genes/Mb
- **CpG island density**: 5.4 per Mb, well below the genome average (~10.5/Mb)
- **Gene count**: ~633 protein-coding genes covering 37% of sequence, but exon coverage is only 1.3% -- one of the lowest among autosomes
- **Average gene length**: 57 kb, nearly double that of chromosomes 6 (31 kb) and 22 (26 kb), reflecting the expansive intergenic and intronic spaces
- **Expression profile**: Chromosome 13 is among the most transcriptionally quiet autosomes; along with chromosomes 4, 8, and 18, it shows significantly decreased overall expression

## Genomic Architecture

### Isochore Structure
Chromosome 13 is dominated by GC-poor isochores (L1 and L2 families), giving it its characteristic gene-sparse, LINE-rich landscape. The low GC content correlates with late replication timing across most of the chromosome, large lamina-associated domains (LADs), and heterochromatic histone marks (H3K9me3). High-GC isochore bands are restricted to a few subtelomeric and pericentromeric regions.

### Repeat Content
- **LINEs**: Heavily enriched relative to genome average, consistent with GC-poor composition. LINE-1 (L1) elements are particularly abundant in the gene desert regions
- **SINEs/Alu**: Relatively depleted compared to gene-rich chromosomes, following the well-established inverse correlation between Alu density and LINE density
- **Total repetitive content**: Approximately 50% of chromosome 13 sequence is repetitive

### Segmental Duplications
Chromosome 13 has one of the lowest percentages of segmental duplications among autosomes on its long arm. However, the short arm (13p) harbors extensive segmental duplications shared with other acrocentric chromosomes -- the T2T project revealed that segmental duplications on acrocentric short arms (34.6 Mbp total across all five) actually exceed both satellite DNA (26.7 Mbp) and rDNA (10 Mbp) in total sequence.

### Common Fragile Sites
- **FRA13A** (13q13.2): Maps within the neurobeachin (NBEA) gene, spanning ~730 kb. Notably situated between two major tumor suppressors -- BRCA2 at 13q13.1 and RB1 at 13q14.2 -- making this region a hotspot for deletions in solid tumors
- **FRA13B**: Additional fragile site contributing to chromosomal instability in cancer genomes

### Gene Deserts
The central region of chromosome 13 contains one of the most dramatic gene deserts in the human genome -- a 38 Mb stretch where gene density drops to 3.1 genes/Mb. A recombination desert spanning 3.7 Mb (D13S1301-D13S233) shows a recombination rate of just 0.16 cM/Mb, approximately one-tenth of the genome average.

## Notable Genes & Loci

### Tumor Suppressors
- **RB1** (13q14.2): The retinoblastoma susceptibility gene -- the first tumor suppressor ever identified (Knudson's two-hit hypothesis, 1971). Loss causes retinoblastoma in children and contributes to osteosarcoma, bladder cancer, and small-cell lung cancer
- **BRCA2** (13q13.1): Encodes a DNA repair protein essential for homologous recombination. Heterozygous germline mutations predispose to breast cancer (lifetime risk ~45-85%), ovarian cancer, pancreatic cancer, and prostate cancer

### MicroRNA Loci
- **miR-15a/miR-16-1** (13q14.3): Located within the DLEU2 locus, immediately adjacent to RB1. Deleted or downregulated in ~68% of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) cases. These tumor-suppressor miRNAs negatively regulate BCL2, and their loss promotes the anti-apoptotic phenotype characteristic of CLL
- **miR-17-92 cluster** (MIR17HG, 13q31.3): The first "oncomiR" -- a polycistronic cluster of six miRNAs (miR-17, miR-18a, miR-19a, miR-20a, miR-19b-1, miR-92a-1). Amplified in B-cell lymphomas and numerous solid tumors. A single chromosome harboring both a classic tumor-suppressor miRNA cluster and a classic oncogenic miRNA cluster is a remarkable duality

### Developmental & Signaling Genes
- **FOXO1** (13q14.11): Forkhead transcription factor rearranged by t(2;13) and t(1;13) translocations in alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma. Spans ~110 kb with a 5.7 kb transcript
- **FLT1** (13q12.3): Encodes VEGFR-1, the receptor for vascular endothelial growth factor
- **EDNRB** (13q22.3): Endothelin receptor type B -- mutations cause Hirschsprung disease (aganglionic megacolon) and Waardenburg-Shah syndrome (deafness + pigmentary defects + Hirschsprung)
- **HTR2A** (13q14-q21): Encodes the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, a primary target of classical psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin) and atypical antipsychotics

### Deafness Gene Cluster
- **GJB2** (13q12.11): Encodes connexin 26 -- mutations are the single most common cause of genetic sensorineural hearing loss worldwide (up to 50% of autosomal recessive cases)
- **GJB6** (13q12.11): Encodes connexin 30, adjacent to GJB2. Large deletions of GJB6 can cause deafness, often in compound heterozygosity with GJB2 mutations

### Other Notable Genes
- **ATP7B** (13q14.3): Copper-transporting ATPase mutated in Wilson disease, causing toxic copper accumulation in liver and brain
- **NBEA** (13q13.2): Neurobeachin -- spans the FRA13A fragile site; implicated in autism spectrum disorders and synaptic function

## Disease Associations

### Trisomy 13 (Patau Syndrome)
- Incidence: ~1 in 5,000-10,000 total births; the least common viable autosomal trisomy
- Median survival: fewer than 3 days; ~90% die within the first year
- 5-year survival rate: 9.7% in population-based studies
- Classic triad: holoprosencephaly, polydactyly, cardiac defects
- Additional features: facial clefting, scalp defects (cutis aplasia), rocker-bottom feet, omphalocele
- **Paradox**: Despite chromosome 13's low gene density (which theoretically means less dosage imbalance), Patau syndrome is more lethal than trisomy 18 (Edward syndrome) and far more lethal than trisomy 21 (Down syndrome). This likely reflects the critical developmental roles of specific 13q genes rather than total gene burden

### Cancer
- **13q14 deletion**: The most common cytogenetic abnormality in CLL, targeting miR-15a/16-1 and RB1
- **BRCA2 mutations**: Major hereditary breast/ovarian cancer gene
- **Retinoblastoma**: Biallelic RB1 inactivation, often via 13q14 deletion
- **Alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma**: t(2;13)(q35;q14) fusing PAX3 with FOXO1

### Robertsonian Translocations
Chromosome 13 is the most frequently involved acrocentric chromosome in Robertsonian translocations. The rob(13;14) translocation accounts for ~75% of all Robertsonian translocations and occurs in approximately 1 in 1,300 individuals. Carriers are phenotypically normal but at risk for unbalanced offspring with trisomy 13 or uniparental disomy.

### Other Genetic Disorders
- **Wilson disease** (ATP7B mutations): Autosomal recessive copper storage disease
- **Nonsyndromic hearing loss** (GJB2/GJB6): Most common genetic cause of congenital deafness
- **Hirschsprung disease** (EDNRB mutations): Congenital absence of enteric ganglia

## Evolutionary History

### Ancestral Conservation
Human chromosome 13 is one of the more conserved autosomes in mammalian evolution. It is maintained as a single syntenic block in the Ancestral Primate Karyotype and can be traced as a recognizable unit across placental mammals. Evolutionary breakpoints cluster between q12.2 and q14 -- the same region enriched for both evolutionary rearrangements and human disease genes.

### Mouse Synteny
In mouse, chromosome 13 sequences map primarily to mouse chromosomes 8 and 14, with smaller segments on mouse chromosome 3. Local gene order is largely preserved within syntenic blocks, despite ~75 million years of independent evolution.

### Primate Evolution
Chromosome 13 is relatively stable across great apes. Chimpanzee chromosome 14 corresponds to human chromosome 13. The acrocentric configuration (with heterochromatic short arm) is conserved across all great apes for this chromosome.

### Ancestral Karyotype
The mammalian ancestor likely had 19 pairs of autosomes. Chromosome 13 as a syntenic unit appears ancestral to at least the eutherian radiation (~100 Mya), though its position and centromere type have varied across lineages.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

### Late Replication Timing
Chromosome 13 is among the latest-replicating autosomes, consistent with its GC-poor, gene-sparse composition. Late replication timing correlates with location in the nuclear periphery (lamina association), condensed chromatin state, and reduced transcriptional activity. The central gene desert replicates particularly late in S-phase.

### Acrocentric Short Arm Structure
The T2T-CHM13 assembly (2022) revealed the detailed architecture of 13p for the first time:
- **Telomere** --> **Distal junction (DJ)** region containing segmental duplications shared with other acrocentric chromosomes
- **rDNA array**: Tandem repeats of ~45 kb units encoding 45S pre-rRNA (18S, 5.8S, 28S). Humans have ~300 rDNA copies total across 5 acrocentric chromosomes
- **Proximal junction (PJ)** region with beta-satellite DNA
- **Centromeric alpha-satellite** arrays
- Satellite III DNA located between the centromere and the NOR

### DNA Secondary Structures
The gene-poor, AT-rich regions of chromosome 13 are relatively depleted for G-quadruplex-forming sequences (which require G-rich tracts) but enriched for AT-rich secondary structures that can form cruciforms and slipped-strand structures. The fragile sites FRA13A and FRA13B represent regions where replication stress can induce non-B DNA conformations and chromosomal breakage.

### Nuclear Positioning
Chromosome 13 territories tend to occupy a more peripheral nuclear position in most cell types, consistent with its heterochromatic character and late replication timing. This peripheral localization brings it into proximity with the nuclear lamina, reinforcing its transcriptionally quiet state.

## Epigenetic Landscape

### Chromatin State
- Dominated by H3K9me3 (constitutive heterochromatin) and H3K27me3 (facultative heterochromatin) marks across its large gene deserts
- The central 38 Mb gene desert is one of the most consistently heterochromatic regions in the human genome across cell types
- Few super-enhancers map to chromosome 13 compared to gene-rich chromosomes

### DNA Methylation
- The GC-poor landscape means fewer CpG sites overall, but those present tend to be heavily methylated in somatic cells
- Late-replicating regions of chromosome 13 show characteristic hypomethylation in cancer cells, where H3K9me3 is replaced by H3K27me3 (constitutive-to-facultative heterochromatin switch)
- The RB1 and BRCA2 promoter CpG islands are classic examples of cancer-associated hypermethylation-mediated silencing

### Replication Timing Domains
Chromosome 13 has some of the largest continuous late-replicating domains in the genome, corresponding to its gene deserts. These late-replicating domains are associated with:
- Lamina-associated domains (LADs)
- Low transcriptional activity
- High LINE density
- Elevated somatic mutation rates in cancer (correlated with late replication)

### NOR Epigenetics
The rDNA arrays on 13p can be either active or silent. Active NORs are marked by the upstream binding transcription factor (UBF/UBTF) and lack DNA methylation, while silent copies carry dense CpG methylation and H3K9me3. The ratio of active to silent rDNA varies between individuals and changes with age, contributing to nucleolar size variation.

## Deep Cuts

1. **The oncomiR duality**: Chromosome 13 is the only human chromosome that harbors both a classic tumor-suppressor miRNA cluster (miR-15a/16-1, deleted in CLL) and the first identified oncogenic miRNA cluster (miR-17-92, amplified in lymphomas). These loci are separated by just ~40 Mb on the same arm.

2. **The gene desert paradox**: Despite having one of the lowest gene densities of any autosome, trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome) is more lethal than trisomy 18 (which has more genes). The severity likely stems from specific developmental master regulators on 13q, not total gene dosage.

3. **The Robertsonian highway**: The rob(13;14) translocation is the single most common balanced chromosomal rearrangement in humans (1:1,300), yet it was only in 2022 that the actual breakpoint sequences in the short arms were resolved by the T2T Consortium. The short arms of acrocentric chromosomes actively recombine with each other -- a process revealed by long-read sequencing to be far more frequent than previously appreciated.

4. **Psychedelic receptor**: HTR2A on chromosome 13 encodes the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor, the primary molecular target of classical psychedelics (LSD, psilocybin, mescaline, DMT). The receptor's location on the genome's most gene-sparse autosome is coincidental but poetic -- the gateway to expanded consciousness sits in a genomic desert.

5. **Connexin deafness hotspot**: The GJB2 gene at 13q12.11 is responsible for more genetic deafness worldwide than any other single locus. The 35delG mutation alone accounts for up to 70% of GJB2-related hearing loss in European populations. The adjacent GJB6 gene harbors a 309-kb deletion (del(GJB6-D13S1830)) that is the second most common cause of genetic deafness in some populations, often acting in trans with GJB2 mutations.

6. **The FRA13A tumor suppressor sandwich**: The common fragile site FRA13A lies precisely between BRCA2 and RB1 -- two of the most important tumor suppressors in human biology. This genomic geography means that large deletions triggered by replication stress at FRA13A can simultaneously inactivate both flanking cancer genes, a double hit from a single event.

7. **Silent rDNA and aging**: The rDNA arrays on 13p progressively lose copies with age, and the proportion of methylated (silent) rDNA copies increases. This age-dependent rDNA silencing contributes to nucleolar shrinkage and reduced ribosome biogenesis -- a process directly linked to cellular senescence and organismal aging.

8. **Short arm recombination**: Long-read sequencing has revealed that the short arms of acrocentric chromosomes (including 13p) undergo frequent recombination with each other during meiosis. This inter-chromosomal exchange means that the short arm of any given chromosome 13 may contain sequences that were, one generation ago, on chromosome 14, 15, 21, or 22. The short arms of acrocentric chromosomes exist in a state of perpetual genomic flux.
