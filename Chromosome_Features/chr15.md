# Chromosome 15

> The imprinting chromosome -- where parent-of-origin rewrites the rules, a single SNP determines eye color for a continent, and segmental duplications make it the most rearrangement-prone acrocentric in the genome.

## Physical Properties

- **Total length**: ~101.99 Mb (GRCh38), the smallest of the three Group D acrocentric chromosomes
- **Centromere**: Acrocentric -- heterochromatic short arm (15p) with rDNA arrays, euchromatic long arm (15q) containing all protein-coding genes
- **Short arm (15p)**: rDNA arrays, satellite DNA, and segmental duplications; sequenced completely for the first time in T2T-CHM13
- **Long arm (15q)**: ~82 Mb of euchromatic sequence
- **Group classification**: Group D (acrocentric), with chromosomes 13 and 14
- **GC content**: 42.2% -- slightly above genome average, and notably higher than chromosome 13 (38.5%). This higher GC content is reflected in relatively greater gene density
- **Gene density**: ~10.3 genes/Mb, above the genome median and far higher than chromosome 13
- **Gene count**: ~849 protein-coding genes
- **Cytogenetic significance**: Chromosome 15 is the most clinically important acrocentric chromosome due to the Prader-Willi/Angelman critical region, the most intensively studied imprinted domain in the human genome

## Genomic Architecture

### The Segmental Duplication Landscape
Chromosome 15 has the most complex segmental duplication architecture of any acrocentric chromosome in its proximal long arm. Five major breakpoint clusters (BP1 through BP5) at 15q11-q13 are defined by large blocks of low-copy repeats (LCRs):
- **BP1-BP2** interval (~0.5 Mb): Contains four non-imprinted genes (NIPA1, NIPA2, CYFIP1, TUBGCP5) deleted in the "Type I" Prader-Willi/Angelman deletion
- **BP2-BP3** interval (~4 Mb): The Prader-Willi/Angelman critical region (PWACR), containing all known imprinted genes
- **BP3-BP4** and **BP4-BP5** intervals: Contain the GABAA receptor gene cluster (GABRB3, GABRA5, GABRG3) and other non-imprinted genes
- These LCRs mediate non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR), making 15q11-q13 one of the most rearrangement-prone regions in the human genome. The recurrence rate for de novo deletions is ~1 in 12,000-20,000 births

### Isochore Structure
The proximal 15q (q11-q14) is moderately GC-rich, while the distal arm shows alternating GC-rich and GC-poor bands. The 15q25-q26 region is particularly gene-rich, containing the CHRNA3/A5/B4 nicotinic receptor cluster and multiple GWAS loci.

### Repeat Content
- **SINEs/Alu**: Enriched relative to genome average, particularly in the proximal 15q region. The segmental duplications at BP1-BP5 contain Alu elements at their boundaries, and Alu-Alu recombination contributes to smaller rearrangements within the PWACR
- **LINEs**: Near genome average overall
- The HERC2 gene at 15q13.1 is itself a segmental duplication -- HERC2 duplicons are scattered across 15q11-q13 and have been a major driver of genomic instability in this region

### Common Fragile Sites
- **FRA15A** (15q22): Aphidicolin-sensitive fragile site
- The proximal 15q region, while not a classical fragile site, is effectively a "structural fragility hotspot" due to the density of segmental duplications

## Notable Genes & Loci

### The Prader-Willi/Angelman Critical Region (15q11.2-q13.1)
The most intensively studied imprinted domain in the human genome:

**Paternally expressed (maternally silenced) genes -- loss causes Prader-Willi syndrome:**
- **SNRPN/SNURF** (15q11.2): Encodes SmN protein (splicing) and the upstream reading frame. The SNRPN promoter CpG island is the canonical DMR for the locus -- methylated on the maternal allele, unmethylated on the paternal allele. This is the standard diagnostic target for methylation-specific PCR/MLPA
- **SNORD116** cluster (previously HBII-85): A cluster of ~29 C/D box snoRNAs. This is now considered the critical gene for the Prader-Willi phenotype -- microdeletions restricted to SNORD116 alone reproduce the full PWS phenotype (hyperphagia, obesity, hypogonadism). The snoRNAs do not have known rRNA targets and likely function in alternative splicing regulation
- **SNORD115** cluster (previously HBII-52): ~48 C/D box snoRNAs with complementarity to the serotonin 5-HT2C receptor mRNA; proposed to regulate alternative splicing of HTR2C, linking imprinting to appetite regulation
- **MKRN3** (15q11.2): Makorin ring finger protein 3; paternally expressed. Loss-of-function mutations are the most common genetic cause of central precocious puberty -- MKRN3 normally acts as a "brake" on GnRH pulse generator activation
- **MAGEL2** (15q11.2): MAGE family member; mutations cause Schaaf-Yang syndrome (neonatal hypotonia, contractures, autism features)
- **NDN** (15q11.2): Necdin; involved in neuronal differentiation and survival
- **IPW**: Imprinted in Prader-Willi; a long noncoding RNA

**Maternally expressed (paternally silenced) gene -- loss causes Angelman syndrome:**
- **UBE3A** (15q11.2): Ubiquitin protein ligase E3A. Encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase that is biallelically expressed in most tissues but exclusively maternally expressed in neurons (brain-specific imprinting). Loss of the maternal copy causes Angelman syndrome. The neuron-specific silencing of paternal UBE3A is mediated by a long antisense transcript (UBE3A-ATS) that originates from the SNURF-SNRPN locus and runs >600 kb through the SNORD clusters to overlap UBE3A. This antisense transcript is only produced from the paternal chromosome

**The Imprinting Center (IC):**
- Bipartite structure: the PWS-IC (centered on the SNRPN promoter) and the AS-IC (~35 kb upstream)
- The PWS-IC maintains paternal gene expression; deletion causes PWS
- The AS-IC resets maternal imprinting; deletion causes AS
- Inherited microdeletions of the IC cause familial recurrence of either PWS or AS

### Pigmentation Loci
- **OCA2** (15q12-q13.1): Encodes the P protein (melanosomal transmembrane transporter). Biallelic loss-of-function causes oculocutaneous albinism type 2, the most common form of albinism in sub-Saharan Africa. OCA2 spans ~345 kb with 24 exons
- **HERC2** (15q13.1): Encodes a giant E3 ubiquitin ligase (~528 kDa, 4,834 amino acids). An intronic SNP in HERC2, **rs12913832** (A>G), is the single strongest genetic determinant of blue vs. brown eye color in humans. The G allele creates a binding site for the transcription factor HECT and RLD domain-containing E3 ubiquitin protein ligase 2, forming a chromatin loop that represses the OCA2 promoter. The blue-eye allele arose approximately 6,000-10,000 years ago, likely in the Black Sea / northwestern European region, and underwent strong positive selection
- The His615Arg variant of OCA2 is separately responsible for lighter skin pigmentation in East Asian populations, reaching allele frequencies of 49-63% in Eastern East Asia

### Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor Cluster (15q25.1)
- **CHRNA5-CHRNA3-CHRNB4**: Three nicotinic receptor subunit genes in a ~200 kb cluster
- The strongest GWAS signal for smoking behavior and nicotine dependence maps here
- rs16969968 (Asp398Asn in CHRNA5) reduces receptor activity; the A allele carriers require more nicotine for equivalent dopamine release
- This same locus is one of the strongest GWAS hits for lung cancer risk -- but the effect is almost entirely mediated through smoking behavior (no association in never-smokers)
- Also associated with COPD risk, alcohol consumption, and cocaine dependence

### Other Notable Genes
- **FBN1** (15q21.1): Encodes fibrillin-1, the major structural component of microfibrils. Mutations cause Marfan syndrome (tall stature, arachnodactyly, aortic root dilation, lens subluxation). FBN1 spans ~235 kb with 65 exons -- over 3,000 different mutations have been identified
- **HEXA** (15q23): Encodes hexosaminidase A alpha subunit; mutations cause Tay-Sachs disease. Carrier frequency reaches 1 in 30 in Ashkenazi Jewish populations
- **CYP1A1/CYP1A2** (15q24.1): Cytochrome P450 enzymes responsible for caffeine metabolism, aryl hydrocarbon metabolism, and drug biotransformation. CYP1A2 polymorphisms determine whether you are a "fast" or "slow" caffeine metabolizer
- **IDH2** (15q26.1): Isocitrate dehydrogenase 2; somatic mutations produce the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate in AML and gliomas, causing genome-wide DNA hypermethylation
- **IGF1R** (15q26.3): Insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor; terminal 15q deletions affecting IGF1R cause intrauterine growth retardation and short stature
- **BLM** (15q26.1): Encodes the Bloom syndrome RecQ helicase; biallelic loss causes Bloom syndrome (growth retardation, immunodeficiency, cancer predisposition, characteristic butterfly facial erythema). Ashkenazi Jewish carrier frequency: 1 in 100
- **PML** (15q24.1): Promyelocytic leukemia protein; fused to RARA by t(15;17) in acute promyelocytic leukemia

## Disease Associations

### Prader-Willi Syndrome (PWS)
- Incidence: ~1 in 15,000-25,000 births
- Caused by loss of paternally expressed genes in 15q11.2-q13: ~70% from 5-6 Mb paternal deletion, ~25% from maternal UPD15, ~2-5% from imprinting defects
- Neonatal phase: severe hypotonia, poor feeding, failure to thrive
- Childhood phase: insatiable hyperphagia, obesity, behavioral problems, mild-moderate intellectual disability, hypogonadism, short stature
- The SNORD116 snoRNA cluster is now considered the critical gene -- microdeletions of SNORD116 alone recapitulate the full PWS phenotype

### Angelman Syndrome (AS)
- Incidence: ~1 in 12,000-20,000 births
- Caused by loss of maternal UBE3A expression in neurons: ~70% from maternal deletion, ~3-7% from paternal UPD15, ~3% from UBE3A point mutations, ~2-4% from imprinting defects
- Features: severe intellectual disability, absent speech (most individuals learn fewer than 5 words), seizures, ataxic gait ("puppet-like" movement), characteristic happy demeanor with frequent laughter, microcephaly
- The happy behavioral phenotype is so distinctive that it was the basis for the syndrome's original (now deprecated) name -- "happy puppet syndrome"

### 15q11-q13 Duplication Syndrome (Dup15q)
- Maternal duplication (interstitial or isodicentric) of the PWACR
- The most common genetic cause of autism, accounting for 1-3% of ASD cases
- Interstitial duplications (trisomy): moderate phenotype with autism, intellectual disability, hypotonia
- Isodicentric 15 (tetrasomy via supernumerary chromosome): severe phenotype with intractable epilepsy, absent speech, and characteristic EEG pattern (high-amplitude beta activity, 20-30 Hz, similar to benzodiazepine effect -- attributed to GABRB3/GABRA5 overexpression)
- **Parent-of-origin effect**: Only maternal duplications cause disease; paternal duplications of the same region are phenotypically silent, because the extra copies contain the same imprinting marks (paternally silenced UBE3A in neurons)

### 15q13.3 Microdeletion Syndrome
- Mediated by NAHR between BP4 and BP5
- 1.5 Mb deletion encompassing CHRNA7 (nicotinic acetylcholine receptor alpha-7 subunit)
- Variable expressivity: intellectual disability, epilepsy (in ~30%), autism, schizophrenia, or apparently normal phenotype
- One of the most common pathogenic CNVs, with ~1% carrier frequency in some psychiatric cohorts

### Cancer
- **t(15;17)(q24;q21)**: PML-RARA fusion in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL). This translocation is both diagnostic and therapeutic -- all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) specifically targets the PML-RARA fusion protein, making APL the first human cancer cured by targeted molecular therapy
- **IDH2 mutations**: Somatic neomorphic mutations (R140Q, R172K) in AML and gliomas produce 2-hydroxyglutarate, which inhibits TET2 and other alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases, causing a DNA hypermethylation phenotype that phenocopies TET2 loss
- **BLM deficiency**: Bloom syndrome patients have ~150-300x increased cancer risk across all types

### Other Disorders
- **Marfan syndrome** (FBN1): Connective tissue disorder with cardiovascular, ocular, and skeletal manifestations; aortic dissection is the leading cause of death
- **Tay-Sachs disease** (HEXA): Lysosomal storage disease causing progressive neurodegeneration; infantile form is fatal by age 3-5
- **Bloom syndrome** (BLM): Genomic instability disorder with elevated sister chromatid exchanges (pathognomonic cytogenetic finding)
- **Oculocutaneous albinism type 2** (OCA2): Most common form of albinism worldwide

## Evolutionary History

### Ancestral Origin
Human chromosome 15 derives from a single ancestral syntenic block that is conserved across placental mammals. In the Ancestral Primate Karyotype, it corresponds to a single unit that has remained largely intact throughout primate evolution.

### Primate Correspondence
- **Chimpanzee**: Human chromosome 15 corresponds to chimpanzee chromosome 16
- **Gorilla/Orangutan**: The syntenic block is preserved with the same acrocentric morphology
- The 15q11-q13 imprinted region is conserved in all eutherian mammals examined, with imprinting of SNRPN, NDN, and UBE3A confirmed in mouse, rat, pig, and cattle

### Mouse Synteny
Human chromosome 15 maps primarily to mouse chromosome 7 (proximal arm, containing the Prader-Willi/Angelman region homolog) and mouse chromosome 9, with smaller segments on other chromosomes. The mouse Prader-Willi region on chromosome 7C shows conserved imprinting and gene order, making the Snrpn-deletion mouse a key model for PWS research.

### The OCA2-HERC2 Evolutionary Story
The OCA2 gene is ancient -- orthologs exist in all vertebrates, where it functions in melanin biosynthesis. The HERC2 intronic enhancer variant (rs12913832) that controls blue eye color arose as a single mutation event ~6,000-10,000 years ago in or near the Black Sea region. This allele then swept through European populations under strong positive selection (the selection coefficient is estimated at s = 0.05-0.10, among the strongest known in recent human evolution). The selective advantage remains debated -- hypotheses include sexual selection, improved vitamin D synthesis at high latitudes, or pleiotropy with other traits.

### SNORD Cluster Expansion
The SNORD116 and SNORD115 snoRNA clusters at 15q11.2 show evidence of rapid evolutionary expansion through tandem duplication. Humans have ~29 SNORD116 copies and ~48 SNORD115 copies, but copy numbers vary across primate species. These brain-expressed snoRNAs appear to have expanded specifically in the primate lineage, coincident with increasing brain complexity. Non-primate mammals have fewer copies, suggesting a role in primate-specific neurodevelopment.

### rDNA Concerted Evolution
Like all acrocentric chromosomes, the rDNA arrays on 15p undergo concerted evolution -- homogenization of repeat sequences across all five acrocentric chromosomes through inter-chromosomal gene conversion and unequal crossing over. This maintains near-identical rRNA coding sequences despite the arrays being physically distributed across five different chromosomes.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

### Segmental Duplication Architecture and NAHR
The five breakpoint clusters (BP1-BP5) at 15q11-q13 are composed of complex segmental duplications containing:
- **HERC2 duplicons**: Copies of the large HERC2 gene have been distributed across the region, creating highly homologous sequences that promote NAHR
- **GOLGA8 family members**: Golgin subfamily A member 8 paralogs embedded within the LCRs
- **END repeats**: Duplicated segments that extend across multiple breakpoints
- The total size of the BP2 and BP3 duplications is ~400 kb each, with >98% sequence identity -- sufficient to support efficient NAHR during meiosis
- The architecture creates a "rearrangement machine" -- the probability of NAHR is proportional to duplication size and identity, and 15q11-q13 has among the highest NAHR rates in the genome

### Chromatin Loop at HERC2-OCA2
The rs12913832 SNP in HERC2 intron 86 controls eye color through a long-range chromatin interaction:
- The G allele (blue-eye allele) reduces binding of the transcription factor to the HERC2 intronic enhancer
- This disrupts a ~200 kb chromatin loop that connects the HERC2 enhancer to the OCA2 promoter
- The loop is mediated by CTCF and cohesin, and its formation/disruption directly modulates OCA2 transcription
- This chromatin organization is conserved in vertebrates, suggesting the loop architecture predates the blue-eye variant by hundreds of millions of years

### The UBE3A-ATS Transcript
The antisense transcript that silences paternal UBE3A in neurons is one of the longest known functional RNA molecules:
- It initiates at the SNURF-SNRPN promoter and extends >600 kb through the entire SNORD cluster region to overlap UBE3A
- Transcription of UBE3A-ATS through the UBE3A gene in the antisense direction is sufficient to silence UBE3A in cis -- a mechanism confirmed by truncation experiments in mouse
- This makes UBE3A-ATS a therapeutic target: antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) that degrade UBE3A-ATS can reactivate the silent paternal UBE3A allele, providing a potential treatment for Angelman syndrome. Multiple clinical trials are underway (GeneTx/Ultragenyx GTX-102, Roche RO7248824, Ionis ION582)

### Nuclear Organization
In neurons, the 15q11-q13 region shows unusual nuclear behavior:
- The Prader-Willi region associates with the nucleolus in some cell types, potentially related to the snoRNA cluster function
- Homologous 15q11-q13 alleles show trans-homolog pairing in human neurons -- an unusual somatic pairing behavior typically associated with Drosophila chromosomes, not mammalian ones

## Epigenetic Landscape

### The PWS/AS Imprinting Center
The most clinically important differentially methylated region (DMR) in the human genome:
- **SNRPN CpG island**: Methylated on the maternal allele, unmethylated on the paternal allele. This methylation difference is established in the gametes and maintained throughout development
- Methylation-specific PCR or MLPA at the SNRPN locus detects ~99% of PWS cases and ~80% of AS cases in a single test
- The imprint is established during gametogenesis: the maternal methylation mark is laid down during oocyte growth, while the paternal allele remains unmethylated in sperm
- **Epigenotype-phenotype correlation**: PWS patients with UPD15 (two maternal copies, both methylated) tend to have milder behavioral phenotypes than those with deletions, possibly because non-imprinted genes in the region are still present in normal dosage

### GABRB3 Epigenetic Regulation
The GABAA receptor beta-3 subunit gene (GABRB3) at 15q12 is not imprinted but shows complex epigenetic regulation:
- Biallelically expressed but with parent-of-origin-dependent expression levels in brain
- Epigenetic dysregulation of GABRB3 has been reported in autism, Rett syndrome, and epilepsy
- The characteristic high-amplitude beta EEG pattern in dup15q syndrome is attributed to GABRB3/GABRA5 overexpression altering GABAergic inhibitory tone

### Replication Timing
- The 15q11-q13 region shows allele-specific asynchronous replication timing, with the paternal allele replicating earlier than the maternal allele in most cell types
- This asynchronous replication is a hallmark of imprinted domains and is established early in development, before transcriptional differences are apparent
- The replication timing difference extends beyond the imprinted domain itself, encompassing the flanking GABAA receptor genes

### DNA Methylation Valleys
Chromosome 15 contains several "DNA methylation valleys" (DMVs) -- extended regions (>5 kb) of constitutive hypomethylation that are conserved across cell types and species. These DMVs typically overlap with developmental transcription factor genes and are marked by broad H3K27me3 domains in non-expressing cells. Notable DMVs on chromosome 15 include those at FOXB1, MEF2A, and HDGFL3.

## Deep Cuts

1. **The snoRNA that controls hunger**: SNORD116, a cluster of 29 small nucleolar RNAs at 15q11.2, is now considered the critical gene for Prader-Willi syndrome's defining feature -- insatiable hunger. These snoRNAs have no known rRNA targets (unlike canonical snoRNAs) and instead appear to regulate alternative splicing of genes involved in hypothalamic appetite circuits. Microdeletions of SNORD116 alone, leaving all other PWS genes intact, reproduce the full hyperphagia phenotype. A noncoding RNA cluster of unknown mechanism is responsible for one of the most devastating appetite disorders in medicine.

2. **One SNP, one continent's eye color**: The rs12913832 variant in HERC2 intron 86 is a single nucleotide change (A>G) that explains 74% of eye color variation in Europeans. The blue-eye G allele disrupts a chromatin loop connecting a HERC2 intronic enhancer to the OCA2 promoter ~200 kb away. This means blue eyes are not caused by a mutation in a pigment gene itself, but by a regulatory variant in an unrelated gene (HERC2 encodes a ubiquitin ligase) that happens to control the chromatin architecture of its neighbor. The variant arose once, ~6,000-10,000 years ago, and every blue-eyed person on Earth descends from a single individual.

3. **The dup15q EEG signature**: Children with isodicentric chromosome 15 (tetrasomy of 15q11-q13) show a distinctive EEG pattern -- persistent high-amplitude beta activity (20-30 Hz) that closely resembles the EEG signature of benzodiazepine administration. This makes neurophysiological sense: benzodiazepines work by enhancing GABAA receptor function, and dup15q patients have 4 copies of the GABRB3/GABRA5 receptor genes. The brain is essentially "on benzodiazepines" from birth due to constitutive GABA receptor overexpression. This also explains why these patients have intractable seizures that paradoxically worsen with benzodiazepine treatment.

4. **The antisense cure**: Angelman syndrome is caused by loss of maternal UBE3A in neurons, while the paternal copy is silenced by a >600 kb antisense RNA (UBE3A-ATS). This creates a unique therapeutic opportunity: if you can destroy the antisense transcript, the silent paternal UBE3A allele wakes up. Multiple clinical trials are now testing antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) injected into the spinal fluid that specifically degrade UBE3A-ATS, reactivating the paternal UBE3A gene. This makes Angelman syndrome one of the few genetic diseases where the "cure" is already in the genome -- you just need to unsilence it.

5. **The caffeine chromosome**: CYP1A2 at 15q24.1 is the primary enzyme responsible for caffeine metabolism. Genetic variation in CYP1A2 determines whether you are a "fast" or "slow" caffeine metabolizer -- slow metabolizers who drink >3 cups of coffee per day have significantly increased risk of myocardial infarction, while fast metabolizers show a protective effect. The same gene also metabolizes theophylline, aromatic amines, and heterocyclic amines from cooked meat, linking chromosome 15 to both your coffee tolerance and your meat-cooking cancer risk.

6. **IDH2 as a TET2 phenocopy**: The IDH2 gene at 15q26.1 is somatically mutated in ~15% of AML and ~80% of lower-grade gliomas. Mutant IDH2 produces the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG), which competitively inhibits TET2 and other alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenases. The result is genome-wide DNA hypermethylation that is biochemically indistinguishable from TET2 loss-of-function. IDH2 mutations and TET2 mutations are mutually exclusive in AML -- they converge on the same epigenetic pathway from opposite directions, one by eliminating the enzyme, the other by poisoning it.

7. **Somatic homolog pairing**: In human neurons, the two copies of 15q11-q13 show trans-homolog pairing -- the maternal and paternal alleles physically associate in the nucleus. This somatic pairing is typically a feature of Drosophila chromosomes and is extremely unusual in mammals. The pairing may be functionally related to the imprinting mechanism, potentially allowing the cell to "compare" maternal and paternal allele states, but its biological purpose remains mysterious.

8. **The HERC2 duplicon rearrangement engine**: The proximal 15q region is riddled with copies of the HERC2 gene -- not just the functional copy at 15q13.1, but at least 7-8 partial HERC2 duplicons scattered across the BP1-BP5 interval. These duplicons are the molecular substrate for the non-allelic homologous recombination that causes PWS/AS deletions, dup15q, and inv dup(15). In a remarkable irony, the very gene whose regulatory variant determines eye color (a benign trait) has, through its evolutionary duplication and dispersal, created the structural framework for some of the most devastating neurodevelopmental disorders in medicine.
