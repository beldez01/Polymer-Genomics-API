# Chromosome 20

> The first metacentric human chromosome to be fully sequenced -- a 64-megabase time capsule of mammalian evolution so perfectly conserved with the mouse that nearly every gene sits in the same order it occupied 87 million years ago.

## Physical Properties

- **Total length**: ~64.4 Mb (GRCh38), representing approximately 2% of the total DNA in cells
- **Centromere type**: Metacentric (one of only 7 human metacentrics), with the centromere positioned near the middle, dividing into a ~27 Mb p-arm and ~37 Mb q-arm
- **Group classification**: Group F (chromosomes 19-20), the smallest metacentrics
- **GC content**: ~44%, slightly above the genome-wide average of 41% -- placing it in the intermediate range, neither as GC-rich as chr19 (48%) nor as AT-rich as chr4 (38%)
- **Gene density**: Moderate. The finished sequence identified 727 genes and 168 pseudogenes across 59.2 Mb of euchromatic sequence (99.4% coverage). This gives a density of ~12.3 genes per Mb, close to the genome average
- **Sequencing milestone**: The euchromatic sequence was completed to "finished" quality (99.4% coverage, <1 error per 100,000 bp) and published by Deloukas et al. in Nature 414, 865-871 (2001) -- the first metacentric human chromosome sequenced to completion

## Genomic Architecture

### The Finished Sequence
The finished chromosome 20 sequence comprises 59,187,298 base pairs. Comparative analysis with mouse (Mus musculus) and pufferfish (Tetraodon nigroviridis) whole-genome shotgun data provided an independent measure of gene annotation efficiency, confirming that the analysis accounted for >95% of all coding exons and nearly all genes. More than 4,800 regions of sequence conservation (RSCs) between mouse and human were detected in intergenic regions alone, many of which likely represent regulatory elements.

### Segmental Duplications
The pericentromeric region of chromosome 20 shows extensive segmental duplication homology with acrocentric chromosome short arms -- chr20 is among the seven chromosomes (1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 16, 20) with the most extensive pericentromeric duplication connections to acrocentric DNA. These duplications create substrates for non-allelic homologous recombination and structural variant formation.

### Repeat Content
- **Total interspersed repeats**: ~44%, near the genome average
- **LINE-1**: Moderate representation (~16%)
- **Alu/SINEs**: Moderate (~12%), substantially lower than chr19's extreme 26%
- **Tandem repeats**: The pericentromeric region contains alpha-satellite arrays typical of metacentric centromeres

### Common Deleted Region at 20q
A recurrent interstitial deletion at 20q11-q13 is the most common cytogenetic abnormality in myeloproliferative neoplasms, affecting ~10% of polycythemia vera patients. The common deleted region (CDR) spans ~2.7 Mb and is presumed to harbor one or more tumor suppressor genes whose loss perturbs normal hematopoiesis. Interstitial deletion (not terminal loss) is the predominant mechanism for 20q LOH.

### Amplification at 20q11.21
Amplifications of genes at chromosome arm 20q are found in 7-9% of colorectal cancers, representing the most commonly amplified locus in this cancer type. The 20q11.21 region shows the highest amplification rate.

## Notable Genes & Loci

### PRNP -- The Prion Protein (20p13)
The gene encoding the prion protein (PrP) sits at the very tip of the short arm. Normal PrP^C is a GPI-anchored glycoprotein expressed in neurons; misfolding into PrP^Sc causes a spectrum of fatal transmissible spongiform encephalopathies:
- **Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD)**: Sporadic, familial, and iatrogenic forms
- **Fatal familial insomnia (FFI)**: Caused by the D178N mutation with methionine at codon 129 -- the same mutation with valine at 129 causes familial CJD. This is one of the most striking examples of a single amino acid context determining disease phenotype
- **Gerstmann-Straussler-Scheinker syndrome (GSS)**: Multiple mutations, including P102L
- **Kuru**: The epidemic prion disease of the Fore people of Papua New Guinea, transmitted by ritual cannibalism

### ADA -- Adenosine Deaminase (20q13.12)
Biallelic mutations cause adenosine deaminase deficiency, accounting for ~15% of all severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) cases and one-third of autosomal recessive SCID. ADA deficiency was the first disease treated by gene therapy (1990, the "bubble boy" protocol by French Anderson and Michael Blaese at the NIH). The ADA locus is historically the most famous gene on chromosome 20.

### JAG1 -- Jagged 1 (20p12.2)
Encodes a ligand for the Notch signaling pathway. Heterozygous loss-of-function mutations cause Alagille syndrome, characterized by bile duct paucity, congenital heart defects (primarily peripheral pulmonic stenosis), butterfly vertebrae, posterior embryotoxon of the eye, and characteristic facies. Approximately 7% of Alagille syndrome cases involve cytogenetically visible 20p12 deletions rather than point mutations.

### GNAS -- The Master Imprinted Locus (20q13.32)
One of the most complex imprinted loci in the human genome. GNAS encodes the alpha subunit of the stimulatory G protein (Gs-alpha), plus multiple alternative transcripts (XLalphas, NESP55, A/B) with distinct imprinting patterns:
- **Maternal allele active (Gs-alpha)**: In renal proximal tubules, thyroid, pituitary, gonads
- **Paternal allele active (XLalphas, A/B)**: In specific tissues
- **Maternal allele only (NESP55)**: Chromogranin-like neuroendocrine protein
- Maternal GNAS mutations cause pseudohypoparathyroidism type 1a (PHP1a) -- PTH resistance plus Albright hereditary osteodystrophy
- Paternal GNAS mutations cause only AHO without hormone resistance
- Imprinting defects (methylation changes at DMRs) cause PHP1b
- Paternal uniparental disomy of the entire chromosome 20 has been documented as a molecular cause of PHP1b

### CHRNA4 -- Nicotinic Acetylcholine Receptor Alpha-4 (20q13.33)
Mutations cause autosomal dominant nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy (ADNFLE, now called autosomal dominant sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy). The Ser284Leu mutation was the first ion channel mutation identified in any form of human epilepsy (Steinlein et al. 1995). Affected individuals experience brief, violent motor seizures arising from sleep.

### SNTA1 -- Alpha-1-Syntrophin (20q11.21)
The A390V mutation causes long QT syndrome type 12 (LQT12) by disrupting the nNOS-SCN5A macromolecular complex. The mutation releases nNOS inhibition, causing S-nitrosylation of the cardiac sodium channel and increasing late sodium current -- an elegant example of a scaffolding protein mutation causing arrhythmia.

### SRC -- Proto-Oncogene Tyrosine Kinase (20q11.23)
The cellular homolog of v-Src, the first oncogene ever identified (Rous sarcoma virus, 1911; Nobel Prize to Peyton Rous in 1966). SRC encodes a non-receptor tyrosine kinase central to cell growth, differentiation, and motility signaling.

### BMP2 -- Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 (20p12.3)
A key TGF-beta superfamily member essential for bone and cartilage formation. Recombinant BMP2 is FDA-approved for spinal fusion and open tibial fractures. Short stature and brachydactyly type A2 have been linked to BMP2 haploinsufficiency.

### ASIP -- Agouti Signaling Protein (20q11.22)
The human homolog of the mouse agouti gene, which controls coat color. ASIP antagonizes melanocortin receptors (MC1R, MC4R) and is associated with skin pigmentation variation in humans. A regulatory variant upstream of ASIP is one of the strongest GWAS signals for skin color across diverse populations.

## Disease Associations

### Prion Diseases
- **Familial CJD, FFI, GSS**: PRNP mutations (20p13), all fatal neurodegenerative diseases
- The codon 129 Met/Val polymorphism modulates susceptibility to sporadic CJD and determines the phenotype of the D178N mutation

### Immunodeficiency
- **ADA-SCID**: First disease treated by gene therapy; now curable with gene-modified autologous stem cell transplantation (Strimvelis, approved 2016)

### Developmental
- **Alagille syndrome**: JAG1 mutations/deletions (20p12) -- multisystem Notch signaling disorder

### Hematological Malignancies
- **del(20q)**: The most common cytogenetic abnormality in polycythemia vera (~10%), also recurrent in MDS and AML. The ~2.7 Mb CDR at 20q11-q13 harbors putative tumor suppressor(s)
- **20q11.21 amplification**: Most commonly amplified locus in colorectal cancer (7-9%)

### Epilepsy
- **ADNFLE/ADSHE**: CHRNA4 mutations (20q13.33) -- first epilepsy-associated channelopathy identified
- **Ring chromosome 20 syndrome**: Formation of a ring chromosome 20 (usually mosaic) causes drug-resistant epilepsy with distinctive prolonged non-convulsive seizures, behavioral problems, and cognitive decline. Unique among ring chromosome syndromes in that epilepsy is the primary manifestation even without gene deletion -- the ring structure itself may disrupt gene expression or chromatin architecture at 20p and 20q telomeric regions

### Endocrine
- **Pseudohypoparathyroidism**: GNAS mutations/imprinting defects (20q13.32) -- parent-of-origin determines phenotype

### Cardiac
- **Long QT syndrome type 12**: SNTA1 mutations (20q11.21)

## Evolutionary History

### Extraordinary Conservation with Mouse
Human chromosome 20 and a portion of mouse chromosome 2 are almost perfectly colinear. The relative position of each of 460 putative coding orthologs was identical in both species, interrupted by only a single small central rearrangement. This near-perfect synteny extends beyond gene order to intron sizes and even the conservation of ancient repetitive sequences. It is one of the most conserved syntenic blocks in the mammalian genome.

### Ancestral Mammalian Form
The human chr20 gene order may represent the ancestral mammalian arrangement: the horse genome also shares an identical marker order with the reconstructed primate ancestor, suggesting this chromosome configuration has been stable for >87 million years since the last common ancestor of Laurasiatheria and Euarchontoglires.

### Primate Lineage Stability
Chromosomes orthologous to human chr20 were not rearranged in the primate lineage. The syntenic block has remained intact through all primate divergences, making it one of the most conserved chromosomes in primates.

### Association with Chromosome 3+21
In the ancestral placental mammalian karyotype, the regions homologous to human chr20 were separate from the chr3+21 association. Chr20 has maintained its independent identity across virtually all mammalian lineages studied.

### Comparative Sequence Conservation
More than 4,800 regions of intergenic sequence conservation were detected between human chr20 and mouse, many located in gene deserts. These conserved non-coding elements (CNEs) are presumed regulatory elements and have been stable for ~87 million years despite having no protein-coding function.

## Biophysical & Structural Features

### Intermediate Mechanical Properties
At ~44% GC, chr20 DNA occupies the middle ground of the biophysical spectrum:
- **Persistence length**: ~55-58 nm (unmethylated), intermediate between AT-rich and GC-rich extremes
- **Melting temperature**: Near-average for the genome
- **Stacking energy**: Moderate -- neither the extreme stability of chr19 nor the flexibility of AT-rich chromosomes
- This intermediate character may contribute to the chromosome's evolutionary stability: neither too rigid nor too flexible, it avoids the extremes that drive structural rearrangement

### Centromeric Architecture
As a metacentric chromosome, chr20 has a well-defined centromere with standard alpha-satellite higher-order repeat arrays. The centromeric region shows the pericentromeric segmental duplications characteristic of metacentrics, with homology to acrocentric short arm sequences.

### Ring Chromosome Formation
Chr20 is notable for forming ring chromosomes at a frequency sufficient to define a clinical syndrome. Ring formation requires breakage at both telomeres and end-joining, suggesting that 20p and 20q subtelomeric regions may have structural features (fragile sites, repetitive elements) that predispose to breakage. The ring chromosome's effect on gene expression -- even without deletion -- points to the importance of linear chromosome topology for normal chromatin function.

### Prion Protein Structural Biology
The PRNP gene at 20p13 encodes a protein whose misfolding is the central event in prion disease. The normal PrP^C structure (predominantly alpha-helical) converts to PrP^Sc (beta-sheet rich) through a conformational change that remains incompletely understood. The 20p13 sequence context of PRNP, including upstream regulatory elements, influences expression levels that modulate disease susceptibility.

## Epigenetic Landscape

### The GNAS Imprinting Hub
The GNAS locus at 20q13.32 is one of the most complex imprinted regions in the human genome:
- **Four differentially methylated regions (DMRs)**: NESP55 DMR (paternal methylation), XL DMR (maternal), A/B DMR (maternal), AS DMR (maternal)
- **Long-range interaction**: Two GNAS imprinting control regions interact over distance to establish the complex tissue-specific imprinting pattern
- **Methylation at the A/B DMR** is the primary imprint established in the oocyte; the other DMRs are secondary (somatic) imprints
- Disruption of any single DMR can cause PHP1b, demonstrating the fragility of the multi-layered imprinting system

### Replication Timing
Chr20 shows a mixed replication timing profile: gene-dense regions replicate early (consistent with euchromatin), while the pericentromeric and some intergenic regions replicate later. The replication timing domains are well-correlated with the gene density profile.

### Chromatin State Distribution
ENCODE chromatin state annotations show chr20 has a balanced distribution of active promoters, enhancers, transcribed regions, and heterochromatin -- more balanced than the extremely euchromatic chr19 or the heterochromatin-dominated large acrocentrics.

### Methylation in Hematological Malignancies
The 20q CDR region shows frequent methylation changes in myeloproliferative neoplasms, with several candidate tumor suppressors (including MYBL2 and L3MBTL1) showing promoter hypermethylation. The epigenetic silencing may complement or substitute for physical deletion.

## Deep Cuts

1. **First metacentric, third overall**: Chr20 was the third human chromosome to be fully sequenced (after chr22 in 1999 and chr21 in 2000), but the first metacentric. Chromosomes 22 and 21 are both acrocentric and small -- chr20 proved that the sequencing methods scaled to larger, structurally different chromosomes.

2. **The gene therapy origin story**: ADA-SCID on chr20 was the target of the first authorized human gene therapy trial in 1990. Ashanthi DeSilva, a 4-year-old with ADA deficiency, received her own T cells modified with a retroviral vector carrying a normal ADA gene. She is still alive today, making this one of the earliest and longest-running gene therapy success stories.

3. **Fatal familial insomnia -- the codon 129 switch**: The PRNP D178N mutation at 20p13 causes two completely different lethal diseases depending on a single polymorphism at codon 129. D178N with Met129 = fatal familial insomnia (selective thalamic degeneration, untreatable insomnia, death in 12-18 months). D178N with Val129 = familial CJD (cortical spongiform degeneration). Same mutation, same gene, different disease -- determined by one amino acid.

4. **The "silent" ring**: Ring chromosome 20 epilepsy is remarkable because most patients have no detectable gene deletion. The ring structure itself -- by disrupting telomere function, altering chromatin looping, or creating position-effect variegation -- is sufficient to cause severe, drug-resistant epilepsy. This makes r(20) one of the best examples of chromosome topology (rather than gene content) determining phenotype.

5. **Agouti at the pigmentation frontier**: The ASIP gene on chr20 is the human counterpart of the mouse agouti locus, which has been studied for over a century as a model of coat color genetics. In humans, regulatory variants near ASIP are among the strongest GWAS signals for skin pigmentation, rivaling MC1R. The agouti system demonstrates that the same gene can control pigmentation across 87 million years of mammalian divergence.

6. **The 4,800 invisible regulators**: The comparative sequencing of chr20 revealed >4,800 conserved non-coding sequences between human and mouse. These regions have been under purifying selection for 87 million years despite having no protein-coding function. Many are presumed enhancers, silencers, or insulators, but the function of most remains unknown -- a vast regulatory dark matter.

7. **SRC: the first oncogene's home**: The SRC proto-oncogene on chr20 is the cellular homolog of v-Src, the transforming element of Rous sarcoma virus -- the first oncogene ever discovered (1911). Peyton Rous received the Nobel Prize in 1966, 55 years after his original finding, one of the longest gaps between discovery and recognition. The gene that launched the oncogene field lives on chr20.

8. **GNAS: the Swiss army knife of imprinting**: The GNAS locus uses alternative promoters, alternative first exons, antisense transcripts, and four DMRs to generate at least five distinct gene products with different imprinting patterns from a single genomic region. It is arguably the most complex imprinted locus in mammals, and its dysfunction produces a bewildering array of clinical phenotypes depending on which transcript is affected and which parental allele carries the mutation.
